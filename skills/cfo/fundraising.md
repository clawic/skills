# Fundraising and Capital

## Timing

Fundraising keys off runway state: 18+ months is strong (raise only opportunistically), 12–17 is healthy (prepare the next round), 6–11 means act now (active raise or cuts that extend past 12), and under 6 is an emergency (cut deep, bridge, or accept dictated terms). The math behind "raise when you can, not when you must": a priced round takes 3–6 months from first deck to wire, so starting at 12+ months of runway means even the slow case closes with leverage intact; starting at 8 means you may negotiate the final terms under 6, and investors read your bank balance in diligence.

- Raise for 18–24+ months of runway: enough to hit the milestones that price the next round, plus a buffer for the raise itself.
- Investors invest in lines, not dots (Mark Suster): consistent monthly updates for a year beat a cold deck — start the updates long before the raise.
- The CFO's job during the raise is to keep the company running and the numbers unimpeachable. If the CFO is also running the process, name who is running the 13-week forecast that week.

## Process Mechanics

- Sequence: build the data room → warm intros in a batch → first meetings compressed into 2–3 weeks → partner meetings → term sheet → confirmatory diligence → docs → wire. Compression is the whole game: a process spread over months looks like a process nobody wanted.
- Track the funnel like a sales pipeline (firm, partner, stage, next step, date). A raise is a sales process where you are the product and the buyer set is finite.
- Term sheet to wire is typically 4–8 weeks of legal and confirmatory diligence. That gap is real cash burn — count it in the runway you claim.
- Signing and closing can be separate, and money arrives on the closing date, not the announcement date. Never plan payroll around a signed term sheet.
- A term sheet is non-binding except for exclusivity/no-shop and confidentiality. The no-shop period is where alternatives die; keep it short.

## Round and Dilution Math

- Post-money = pre-money + amount raised. Raise $5M at $20M pre → $25M post → new investor owns 20%; everyone else is diluted by 20% proportionally.
- Typical dilution per priced round: 15–25%. Two rounds at 20% each leave founders with 64% of their starting stake before any option pool.
- **Option pool shuffle**: investors usually require the pool topped up pre-money, so existing holders pay for it. A "$20M pre" with a 10% post-money unallocated pool demand is effectively ~$18M for current shareholders. Counter: size the pool from a named 12–18 month hiring plan, not the investor's round number, and count shares returned from departures before asking for any increase.
- Model before every round: current ownership → new shares → post-money ownership → next-round assumptions → exit scenarios. A cap-table spreadsheet with scenario tabs; recompute when any term changes.

## SAFEs and Convertible Notes

- A SAFE is future equity, not debt, and it dilutes at conversion — not on the day you sign it. Plan every round on the **as-converted** cap table or the founder ownership number in your deck is fiction.
- Post-money SAFEs (the common standard) fix the investor's percentage: dilution from each new SAFE lands entirely on existing holders, and stacking several is how founders arrive at a priced round owning far less than they believed. Recompute the fully diluted table after every SAFE signed.
- Valuation cap vs discount: the investor gets the better of the two at conversion. A cap well below the eventual priced round is a large, silent transfer of ownership.
- Notes add a maturity date and interest — a real deadline that can force a conversion or an extension negotiation at the worst moment. Track maturity dates in the same place as covenant test dates.
- Pro-rata side letters and MFN clauses travel with these instruments and constrain the next round's allocation. Keep a single list of every side letter ever signed; buyers and lead investors will ask.

## Term Sheet Terms

| Term | What it means | Market standard | Red flag |
|------|---------------|-----------------|----------|
| Liquidation preference | Who is paid first at exit | 1x non-participating | >1x, or participating preferred |
| Anti-dilution | Protection in a down round | Broad-based weighted average | Full ratchet |
| Board | Control and governance | 2 founders / 1 investor (or 2-2-1) at seed–A | Investor majority pre-B |
| Option pool | Employee equity dilution | Sized to hiring plan, pre-money | Arbitrary 15%+ demand |
| Pro-rata rights | Follow-on rights | Standard for major investors | Super pro-rata |
| Protective provisions | Investor veto list | Narrow: new preferred, sale, charter changes | Vetoes over budget or hiring |
| Tranched milestones | Money released on targets | Rare, avoid | Any tranching — it's an option, not a commitment |

Valuation is the term founders over-negotiate; preference stack and board control are the terms that decide who gets paid and who gets fired. Trade valuation for clean terms, not the reverse.

## Down Rounds and Bridges

- A down round with clean terms beats a flat round with structure. Structure (participating preferred, ratchets, senior stacks) is deferred dilution with interest — it detonates at exit, when you can least renegotiate.
- Reprice fast, communicate the plan, and reset employee options in the same motion. A slow-motion down round demoralizes twice.
- An insider bridge is the cheapest emergency capital and the loudest signal: existing investors funding at the last price are buying time; existing investors declining to bridge is the information your next investor will act on.
- Anti-dilution adjustments and a new 409A both follow a down round. Run the recalculated cap table — fully diluted, every SAFE as converted — before agreeing anything.

## Data Room

Ready before the first partner meeting — assembling it mid-process telegraphs disorganization and slows momentum when it's most expensive:

**Required**: financial statements (since inception), monthly P&L and balance sheet, fully diluted cap table, 12 months of bank statements, revenue by customer, key contracts, employee roster with compensation.
**Strengthens the raise**: cohort analysis, unit economics, pipeline, roadmap, competitive analysis.

What diligence probes first — pre-answer these in the room:
- Customer concentration: any customer above ~20% of revenue gets a dedicated question; above ~30% it prices the round.
- Revenue recognition: annual contracts booked as monthly revenue is the classic startup restatement.
- Uncollected sales tax, contractor misclassification, and unsigned IP assignments — the three findings that reliably appear.
- Related-party transactions and verbal side letters: disclose before they're found.

## Investor Updates

Monthly, especially when it's hard — silence is read as bad news at maximum volume.

```
Subject: [Company] Monthly Update - [Month Year]

TL;DR: [One sentence state of company]

Key Metrics: MRR $X (+Y% MoM) · Customers X (+Y) · Runway X months
Wins: [2-3, concrete]
Challenges: [each with what you're doing about it]
Asks: [specific intros or help]
```

Same metrics, same definitions, every month. Changing how you calculate a metric mid-stream reads as hiding something — if a definition must change, flag it and restate history. Check what your financing documents contractually require — information rights define which investors get what, on what deadline — before deciding what to send to whom.
