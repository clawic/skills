# Headcount, Compensation, and the Hiring Plan

People are 60–80% of operating spend at most software companies, and the only spend category that resists being reversed. Every headcount decision is a runway decision.

## Fully Loaded Cost

```
Fully loaded monthly cost = base salary × 1.25–1.4 ÷ 12  (+ one-time recruiting cost)
```

The multiplier covers employer payroll taxes, benefits, equipment, software seats, and workspace; it varies by country far more than by seniority. Modeling raw salaries understates true people cost by **20–29%** of the loaded figure (0.25/1.25 to 0.4/1.4) — the error appears exactly when hiring accelerates. Agency recruiting adds a one-time fee, commonly a fifth to a quarter of first-year base, in the month of the start date.

## Start Dates Are the Budget Killer

The classic overrun is not overhiring — it is assuming everyone starts on January 1.

- Senior roles typically take 2–3 months from opening a requisition to an accepted offer, plus the candidate's notice period. A role opened in January produces a start date in April at best.
- Model the plan twice: **plan starts** and **realistic starts**. The difference is the hiring capacity assumption you are making without saying so.
- Slippage cuts both ways: it saves cash and misses the capacity the revenue plan assumed. Report both effects, never just the favorable one.

## Ramp

- Revenue-generating hires produce nothing during ramp; count **ramped** reps only in the driver model. A rep who starts in March contributes little before the second half.
- Engineering ramp is real too — new engineers are typically net-negative in their first weeks because they consume senior time. A hiring wave slows delivery before it speeds it up.

## From Plan to Requisition

Budget headcount is not approved headcount. Each line in the plan carries:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Role and level | Determines band and equity range |
| Assumed start month | Drives the burn forecast |
| Trigger | The metric or milestone that unlocks the requisition (e.g. "opens when ARR passes X") |
| Owner | Who is accountable for the hire and its output |
| Kill condition | When the plan misses, this line closes without a debate |

Trigger-gated hiring is the mechanism that makes a downside scenario executable: revenue misses, requisitions do not open, and nobody had to re-argue the budget.

## Compensation Bands

- Pick one benchmark source and one target percentile per level; publish the band width (a common structure is roughly ±15% around the midpoint) and hold it. Ad-hoc offers create pay compression that costs more to unwind than the original hire saved.
- Geographic differentials: decide once between a single global band and location-adjusted bands, and write down which market each location is benchmarked to. Changing this policy later triggers pay cuts nobody will accept.
- Refresh bands annually with the comp cycle; equity ranges per level come from the pool plan.
- Counter-offers above band are a policy decision, not a hiring-manager decision. Every exception becomes the new floor.

## Contractor vs Employee

- The test is **control**, not the contract: who sets the hours, provides the tools, directs the method, and whether the work is core to the business. A contract calling someone a contractor proves nothing.
- Misclassification cost is back payroll taxes plus penalties and interest, and it is a standard diligence finding — an acquirer will price it.
- Legitimate contractor uses: bounded projects, specialist work, genuine part-time. For a foreign hire who works like an employee, use an employer of record rather than a contractor agreement.

## Payroll Cost Is Seasonal

- Employer social-security-style contributions with an annual wage ceiling reset each January, so Q1 payroll cost is structurally the highest quarter of the year for a high-salary team, and Q4 the lowest. Unemployment contributions are front-loaded the same way.
- A flat monthly payroll assumption therefore overstates Q4 burn and understates Q1 — precisely when a January board meeting reviews runway. Build the forecast on the payroll provider's actual schedule.
- Bonuses, commission accelerators, and the annual comp increase all land in predictable months; put each on its own line rather than smoothing them.

## Attrition and Backfills

- Planning at zero attrition is wrong in both directions: it overstates cost (people leave) and understates hiring load (backfills compete with new roles for recruiter capacity). Use the company's own trailing rate; if there is no history, state the assumption you used.
- Backfill approval should be a decision, not a reflex. The question is whether the role would be opened today from scratch.
