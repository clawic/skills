# Being Acquired, and Acquiring

Headline price is the number everyone remembers and rarely the number anyone receives. The mechanics below decide the difference between the two.

## The Process, and Where Leverage Lives

Outreach or inbound → indication of interest → **letter of intent** → exclusivity → confirmatory diligence → definitive agreement → close.

- Leverage peaks the moment before exclusivity is signed and drops to near zero afterwards. Negotiate price, the working-capital mechanism, escrow, and the treatment of employee equity **in the LOI**, not in the definitive agreement.
- Keep exclusivity short (a stated number of weeks) with a hard expiry, and keep alternatives warm until signing. A retrade — the buyer reducing price late on diligence findings — works because the seller has no alternative left.
- Run a real process if you want a real price: a single bidder sets the price alone.
- Expect 3–6 months from LOI to close for a private deal, longer with regulatory review, and expect the founders' attention to be consumed for the duration. Someone other than the CEO must run the company while this happens.

## Enterprise Value Is Not What You Receive

```
Equity value = enterprise value + cash − debt − debt-like items − transaction costs
             ± working-capital adjustment
```

Debt-like items are where the surprises live: accrued and unpaid taxes, accrued leave balances, deferred compensation and bonuses, capital leases, unfunded severance, customer prepayments in some deals, and unpaid transaction expenses. Every one of these reduces the wire. List them yourself before the buyer does — a debt-like item you disclose is a negotiation; one they find is a price cut.

## The Working Capital Peg

- The buyer expects the business delivered with a normal level of working capital. The peg is usually the trailing 12-month average net working capital, trued up 60–90 days after close.
- Every dollar of peg is a dollar of price. This is negotiated less than valuation and moves outcomes more.
- Watch the definition: whether deferred revenue and cash are inside or outside the calculation can change the number materially. And do not manage cash strangely in the months before close — the average is measured over a period you have already lived.

## Escrow, Indemnity, and Insurance

- A holdback or escrow securing the seller's reps has historically run around 10% of price for 12–18 months, with a separate special indemnity for a known problem.
- Representation and warranty insurance has changed the default on mid-market deals: the buyer's recourse shifts to the policy, leaving a small retention, in exchange for a premium priced as a percentage of the coverage limit. Whether the buyer, seller, or both pay is negotiable and worth negotiating.
- Fundamental reps (title, capitalization, taxes) survive longer than operational reps and are usually uncapped or capped high. Cap-table accuracy is therefore a personal-exposure question for founders, not an admin one: every grant, exercise, and transfer needs a signed consent behind it.

## Earnouts

- An earnout is not price; it is an option written by the buyer. Treat the deal value as the cash at close plus whatever probability you honestly assign.
- If one is unavoidable, negotiate the mechanics, not the target: which accounting policies apply post-close, who controls the levers (headcount, pricing, product roadmap, the sales team), what happens on a change of control during the earnout, and what reporting you receive. Disputes cluster on post-close changes to how the metric is computed.
- Prefer a metric the seller can still influence and the buyer cannot easily redefine — revenue over any margin measure, since costs become the buyer's after close.

## What Diligence Will Find

Fix these before the process rather than during it:

| Finding | Why it is priced |
|---|---|
| Revenue recognized ahead of delivery | Restatement risk; every metric derived from it becomes suspect |
| Uncollected sales tax or VAT in nexus states | Quantifiable liability plus penalties; usually escrowed |
| Contractor misclassification | Back taxes and penalties, and it recurs monthly until fixed; the test is control, not the contract |
| Missing IP assignment agreements | The buyer may not own what it is paying for; a genuine deal-breaker |
| Cap-table errors, unsigned consents, stale 409A | Delays closing and can require remediation grants |
| Customer concentration above ~20–30% of revenue | Prices the round or the deal; above the higher end it changes the structure |
| Related-party transactions and verbal side letters | Trust damage disproportionate to the amount |
| Change-of-control or assignment clauses in key contracts | Consents needed from customers who now have leverage |

## Employees in the Deal

- **Acceleration**: single trigger (vesting accelerates on change of control) vs double trigger (change of control plus termination). Buyers prefer double; the difference is retention, and it is settled in the LOI.
- Unvested equity is typically assumed or cashed out on a schedule; vested options are cashed out **net of strike and withholding**, which is smaller than employees expect. Tell them the mechanics before the number circulates informally.
- Retention pools for key employees usually come out of the purchase price, diluting sellers. Negotiate whether the pool sits above or below the price line.
- Communication timing is a legal question as well as a cultural one. Nothing goes to the team without counsel's sequence.

## Buy-Side

- Price the integration, not the target: onboarding, systems migration, duplicate tooling, retention packages, and the leadership attention consumed for two quarters.
- Acqui-hire arithmetic is simple and rarely done: cost per retained engineer including retention and severance for those who leave, compared against recruiting the same team directly.
- For acquisitions in fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2022, acquired contract liabilities are measured under the revenue standard rather than at fair value, which largely removed the old deferred-revenue haircut that used to erase post-close revenue. Confirm with the accountant before modeling combined revenue.
- Pay for what already works. A thesis requiring the target to perform better after acquisition than before is a plan, not a valuation.
