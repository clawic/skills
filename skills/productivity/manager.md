# Manager — Your Work Happens Between Everyone Else's

The manager constraint: the job is a queue of interruptions by design, and the individual work is what gets sacrificed. Graham's distinction is the frame — a manager's day is split into slots and a maker's day needs half of one — and a manager who also owns delivery work is living in both schedules at once, which is why the delivery work slips.

**Before advising**, read `## Constraints`, `## Commitments` and `## Due` in `~/Clawic/data/productivity/memory.md`, and the shared `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md` for the team. A forgotten 1:1 commitment costs more trust than a missed deadline.

## What Fails Here

- **"My door is always open."** Read as an invitation to interrupt rather than an offer of support; the team learns to ask instead of deciding, and the queue grows to fill the availability.
- **Meeting colonization.** Back-to-back 1:1s, syncs and alignments leave no interval long enough for anything but reaction (`meetings.md`).
- **"It's faster if I do it."** True once, false forever after. Each repetition raises the team's dependency and lowers their range.
- **Unclear delegation.** No criteria, no authority level, no date — the work returns needing more time than doing it would have taken (`delegation.md`).
- **Context-switch churn.** Ten thirty-minute switches produce exhaustion without a single completed thing; the resumption cost is paid ten times.
- **Managing by exception only.** Talking to people only when something is wrong makes every conversation a threat and kills the early signals.

## What Works

- **Office hours, not an open door.** A published window — "2-4pm daily for anything" — gives the team faster real access than an open door does, because the rest of the day stops being a gamble.
- **Batch the people work.** All 1:1s on one or two days. Two whole days beat five fragmented ones, and the team adapts within a week.
- **A manager block.** 2 hours weekly for planning, writing, hiring and thinking — treated as an external commitment (`planning.md`).
- **Decision frameworks over decisions.** "If under X, decide yourself; if over X, tell me first." Each one converts a stream of escalations into a rule (`delegation.md`).
- **Delegate outcomes with an authority level stated.** Level 1-5, said out loud. Most rework comes from the delegator meaning 3 and the person hearing 5.
- **Five minutes of 1:1 prep.** Last commitments, what they raised, one thing to notice. It is the difference between a status report and a conversation that surfaces problems early.

## The Leverage Test

Ask of every hour: does this multiply the team's output, or add one person's worth of output? Coaching, unblocking, hiring and criteria-setting multiply. Doing the work adds one. Managers who add for a whole quarter tend to have a team quietly waiting on them for the entire quarter.

Corollary that is easy to miss: a manager doing IC work is also occupying the growth opportunity of whoever should be doing it. The cost is not only your hours, it is their range next year.

## 1:1s

- Weekly for new, struggling or fast-changing roles; biweekly for stable senior ones. Below biweekly it stops being a channel where problems surface early.
- Their agenda first. A manager-driven 1:1 turns into a status report, and status is better read than performed.
- Never cancel; move. A cancelled 1:1 is read as a ranking, whatever was said.
- Every commitment made in a 1:1 goes to `## Commitments` in the same turn, with direction and the person's name. Forgetting what you promised in a 1:1 is the fastest available trust leak.
- Once a quarter, ask the two questions the weekly rhythm never reaches: what do you want to be doing in a year, and what is the most frustrating part of this job right now.

## People Problems Cost Differently

- A people problem drains for hours after it ends; a task problem does not. Do not schedule demanding solo work immediately after a hard conversation — schedule the recovery instead (`energy.md`).
- "I need to think about this and come back tomorrow" is a complete answer, and it usually produces a better one.
- The team reads your state. A visibly overloaded manager produces a team that hides problems, because nobody wants to add to the pile.
- Protecting your own time openly is the permission structure the team needs to protect theirs. What you model is the policy; what you write is a suggestion.

## Managing Up While Managing Down

- Bring your manager the queue, not the complaint: "here is what is in flight, I can do two of these three — which two?" (`delegation.md`).
- Report outcomes and risks, not activity. Activity reports invite management of your calendar; outcome reports invite trust.
- Escalate early, with a recommendation. An escalation that arrives with options and a preference is a decision request; one that arrives without is a delegation upward.

## The Real Issue

Manager productivity problems are usually systems problems: unclear ownership across the team, too many recurring meetings, and delegation without authority. Fixing your own schedule inside a broken system buys a few hours; fixing ownership and criteria buys the team's whole week — and it is the part of the job only you can do.

## What to Write Down

- Standing team meetings, review cycles, on-call and hiring loops go to `## Constraints`; the recurring-meeting audit is a `## Due` row (`meetings.md`).
- Every 1:1 commitment goes to `## Commitments`, with the person's row in `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md` — name, role, channel, one line of working context. Never performance judgments, health, or personal circumstances.
- Decision frameworks and delegation briefs go to `~/Clawic/data/productivity/artifacts/` with their `## Boxes` lines; they are what makes the next quarter cheaper.
- Team deliverables you own become project files in the shared `~/Clawic/data/projects/`.
- A recurring escalation pattern (the same decision arriving weekly) goes to `## Friction` — it is a missing criterion, and writing the criterion removes it permanently.
