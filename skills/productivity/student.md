# Student — Deadlines, Exams, and a Calendar Full of Holes

The student constraint: enormous unstructured time, almost no external accountability between deadlines, and an evaluation system that rewards a burst at the end. Advice written for office workers assumes a schedule that already exists; here the schedule has to be manufactured.

**Before advising**, read `## Constraints`, `## Energy Patterns` and `## Tasks` in `~/Clawic/data/productivity/memory.md`. Term dates, exam weeks and contact hours are constraints, and every plan below is built on them.

## What Fails Here

- **The semester plan made in week 1.** It assumes uniform load across a term whose real shape is two spikes. It dies in week 3 and takes the user's belief in planning with it.
- **Deadline-driven panic as a strategy.** Things do get submitted. It also removes the revision pass and the sleep, and it trains a dependency that breaks the moment two deadlines coincide — which happens every term, because course designers do not coordinate.
- **The all-day library session.** Twelve hours in a building yields 2-4 hours of real work for most people; the rest is presence. Hours-in-seat is the metric students report and the one that correlates worst with results.
- **Rereading and highlighting.** They produce familiarity, not recall. Familiarity is exactly what makes an exam surprising.
- **Perfectionism on the cheap parts.** Six hours on citation formatting while the argument is weak. The rubric usually says where the marks are; almost nobody reads it twice.

## What Works

- **Build the term's shape in week 1**: every deadline and exam on one page, then count backwards. Two deadlines in the same week is a thing to find in September, not in November — moving one is only possible early.
- **Work before the class that covers it.** Material is fresh, questions are live, and the hour has a natural boundary.
- **Retrieval over review.** Closed-book recall, practice questions, past papers. Testing yourself is the study method with the strongest evidence behind it, and it is unpleasant precisely because it reveals what you do not know.
- **Spaced, not massed.** The same total hours spread over five days beat one long session for anything that must survive to an exam. Cramming works for tomorrow and is gone the following week.
- **One-pomodoro start.** 25 minutes, then reassess. Starting is the whole battle when nothing is due tomorrow (`procrastination.md`).
- **Weekend morning rule.** Two hours Saturday morning, then the weekend is genuinely free — a structure that produces both output and rest, unlike the standard version, which produces neither.
- **Groups for showing up, alone for understanding.** Study groups are excellent accountability and mediocre learning environments; use both deliberately.

## Working Backwards From a Deadline

Formula for anything with a submission date: `start date = deadline − (estimated hours × ratio ÷ hours available per day) − 2 buffer days`.

A 20-hour essay, ratio 1.6, 3 available hours a day: 32 ÷ 3 ≈ 11 working days, plus 2 buffer days = start 13 days out. The buffer is not politeness; it absorbs the printer, the flu, and the source that turns out not to say what you remembered.

Checkpoints beat one deadline: outline at 25% of the elapsed time, rough draft at 60%, revision at 85%. Three checkpoints produce three starts; one deadline produces one panic.

## Exam Weeks

- Build the revision schedule backwards from exam dates, weighted by the weight of the paper and by current weakness — not by which subject is pleasant.
- Practice under exam conditions at least once per subject: timed, closed-book, handwritten if the exam is handwritten. The gap between knowing and performing is its own skill.
- Sleep is a study technique in exam week, not a competitor to it: consolidation happens during sleep, and under ~7 hours the recall being trained degrades (`energy.md`).
- The last 24 hours are for light retrieval and logistics. New material that late mostly displaces confidence.

## Group Work

- Get the split, the deadlines and the channel in writing at the first meeting. Group failure is nearly always an unstated assumption about who owns what.
- Track what others owe you in `## Commitments` as `owed to me`, with names in the shared contacts box. Chasing from memory two days before submission is how group projects end badly.
- Integrate early with a deliberately rough version. Discovering at hour 3 that two sections contradict each other is survivable; discovering it at hour 30 is not.

## The Real Issue

Most student productivity problems are fear problems in a scheduling costume: fear of starting and confirming you do not understand it. That fear is what makes the library hour feel productive and the practice question feel impossible — it is why retrieval practice, the thing that works, is the thing that gets avoided. Name it, then shrink the first action until it sits beneath the fear (`procrastination.md`).

## What to Write Down

- Term dates, contact hours, exam weeks and any job or care schedule go to `## Constraints` — every plan reads from it.
- Each course or multi-step deliverable becomes a project file in the shared `~/Clawic/data/projects/`, with one next action in `## Tasks`.
- Estimate and actual for essays and problem sets go to `## Calibration` as a pair. A student's ratio is usually higher than they expect, and knowing it changes every start date.
- Group commitments go to `## Commitments`; teammates to `~/Clawic/data/contacts/contacts.md` with name, role and channel only.
- What works for this person — retrieval format, best study hours, which subjects trigger avoidance — goes to `## How They Work` and `## Energy Patterns`.
