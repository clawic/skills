### Beijing Accent

Beijing Mandarin adds "r" endings (儿化 érhuà). If your textbook phrase gets blank stares, try the -r form:

| English | Standard | Beijing |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Where? | 哪里 nǎlǐ | 哪儿 nǎr |
| A little | 一点 yīdiǎn | 一点儿 yīdiǎnr |
| Here | 这里 zhèlǐ | 这儿 zhèr |

## Face (面子) — Operational Version

Skip the theory; these are the moves:

- Praise publicly, criticize privately — including with landlords, agents, and colleagues. A public complaint hardens the other side's position instead of fixing the problem.
- Never force a yes/no answer. Leave an ambiguous exit ("maybe another time") and let the other side take it.
- **Read deferral as refusal**: "we'll see" (再说), "maybe" (可能), "a bit inconvenient" (不太方便), or a topic change usually mean NO. Pushing for an explicit no costs face on both sides and burns the relationship.
- If someone makes a mistake that affects you, give them a face-saving script ("maybe the system was down?") — you'll get faster correction than by assigning blame.

## Guanxi (关系) — Operational Version

- Favors are a running ledger. Accepting significant help creates a real obligation to reciprocate — decline politely if you can't or won't repay.
- Introductions carry the introducer's face. If someone connects you, your behavior reflects on them; report back and thank them.
- Banquet and KTV invitations from business contacts are relationship work, not leisure. Declining twice without a counter-invitation reads as disinterest.
- Cold outreach underperforms badly. For anything hard (apartment disputes, bureaucracy shortcuts, hiring), ask "who do I know who knows someone" first.

## Gifts

| Situation | Give | Never give |
|-----------|------|-----------|
| Business meeting | Quality item from your home country | Clocks ("giving a clock" 送钟 sounds like attending a funeral 送终) |
| Home visit | Fruit, wine, chocolate | Sharp objects (cutting ties), pears (离 separation) |
| Chinese New Year | Red envelope (hongbao) | White or black wrapping (funerals) |
| Thank you | Tea, specialty food | Green hats (marks the wearer as cuckolded) |

- Offer and receive with two hands. Expect 2-3 polite refusals before acceptance — persist gently.
- Recipients don't open gifts in front of the giver unless invited to.
- Digital hongbao (WeChat): symbolic amounts beat round numbers — 6.66, 8.88, 5.20 ("I love you") read as thoughtful; anything with 4 reads as tone-deaf.
- Amounts: avoid 4 in any gift of money (sounds like "death"); 8 is the lucky default.

Business cards: give and receive with two hands, look at it before putting it away, never write on it in front of the giver. Still expected in formal meetings despite WeChat.

## Dining Etiquette

### The Meal Script

1. Seating is assigned by status: the seat facing the door is the host's or guest of honor's — wait to be placed.
2. The host orders for the table. Dishes ≈ number of people + 1-2, shared from the center. If asked to add a dish, pick a modest one — ordering the most expensive item is a status claim.
3. Rice arrives LAST (it's the filler, not the base). Asking for it early signals the food isn't enough.
4. Try everything offered; leave a little on shared plates at the end — a wiped-clean table implies the host under-ordered.
5. Bones and shells go on the table or side plate — normal, not rude.
6. The bill: the host pays; guests perform a fight for it. Let the host win, then reciprocate by hosting next time. Splitting the bill (AA制) is fine among young colleagues, never at a formal invitation.

### Drinking Culture

| Phrase | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| 干杯 gān bēi | "Dry glass" — you're expected to finish it |
| 随意 suí yì | "As you wish" — a sip is fine |
| Toast lower | Clink with your rim below a senior's = respect |
| Refill others first | Filling only your own glass is a visible slight |

Escape scripts that work without offense: "I'm driving" (我开车), health/doctor's orders (身体不好), or toast with tea — raise it with the same ceremony. What doesn't work: silently not drinking; you must visibly participate in toasts.

Chopstick taboos (all funeral-associated): never upright in rice, never pass food chopstick-to-chopstick, don't point or spear.

## WeChat Etiquette

- Add contacts by scanning QR in person or via introduction; cold-adding strangers gets rejected.
- Voice messages: fine with friends and elders, rude to busy strangers or business contacts (they must play audio to triage). Text first.
- Moments (朋友圈) is semi-public: colleagues, landlords, and officials may see posts. Never post political content (→ Sensitive Topics).
- Work chat leans formal; group chats have hierarchies — let seniors speak first on decisions.

## Cultural Calendar

| Holiday | When | Practical impact |
|---------|------|------------------|
| Chinese New Year (春节) | Jan/Feb (lunar) | 1-2 weeks: shops/restaurants close, migrant workforce leaves, intercity travel sells out. Don't schedule anything. |
| Qingming (清明) | Early April | 3 days, cemeteries busy, manageable |
| Labor Day (劳动节) | May 1-5 | 5 days, tourist sites packed |
| Dragon Boat (端午) | June (lunar) | 3 days, manageable |
| Mid-Autumn (中秋) | Sep/Oct (lunar) | 3 days; give mooncakes to colleagues/clients |
| National Day "Golden Week" (国庆) | Oct 1-7 | 7 days: worst crowds of the year, hotel surge pricing |

Note: holidays come with "makeup workdays" — the adjacent Saturday/Sunday may be a working day. Check the official calendar before scheduling.

Numbers: 8 = wealth (lucky), 6 = smooth, 9 = lasting; 4 = death (avoid in prices, floors, gifts, phone numbers).

## Sensitive Topics

Never raise, and deflect if raised: Taiwan, Tibet, Xinjiang, Tiananmen, Hong Kong protests, Party criticism, comparisons with Japan's wartime history. This applies on WeChat too — it is monitored, and a "joke" in a group chat can have visa consequences for you and legal ones for locals present. Safe and welcome: food, travel within China, tech, family, hobbies.

Personal questions — age, salary, marriage, why no children — are friendliness, not intrusion. Vague cheerful answers are completely acceptable.

## Daily Rhythm & Norms

- Business meetings: arrive on time or early. Dinner invitations: 10-15 min late is normal. Government offices: arrive before opening, expect queues.
- Lunch is early (11:30-12:30) and sacred; many offices go quiet, some nap (午休). Don't schedule calls at noon.
- Public behavior that surprises Westerners but is normal: staring at foreigners (curiosity), loud phone calls, spitting (declining), queue-cutting (guard your spot without outrage).
- Service is transactional, not chatty; delivery and convenience are world-class — default to delivery for anything annoying to do in person.

## What Actually Builds Relationships Here

1. 差不多 (chà bù duō, "close enough") is the operating philosophy — match it; precision-demands read as distrust.
2. Reciprocate every hosted meal within a few weeks.
3. Genuine effort in Mandarin — even 20 phrases — outperforms fluent English everywhere outside Sanlitun.
4. Show up to colleague activities (team dinners, hikes); attendance IS the relationship.
5. Knowing basic Chinese history (dynasties, the century of humiliation narrative) turns small talk into real conversation.
