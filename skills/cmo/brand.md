# Brand — Assets, Budget, Architecture, Tracking

Positioning is what you mean; brand is what gets remembered when nobody is paying attention. Positioning work lives in `positioning.md`; this file covers the asset, the budget, the structure, and the measurement.

## Mental and Physical Availability

Ehrenberg-Bass (Byron Sharp): buyers choose among the brands they can recall at the moment of buying, in the places they can buy. Two consequences that change budgets:

- **Penetration beats loyalty for growth.** Brands grow mainly by adding light and non-buyers, not by deepening the enthusiasm of existing fans. A loyalty program is a retention lever, not a growth strategy.
- **Double jeopardy**: smaller brands have both fewer buyers *and* slightly lower loyalty. Chasing the loyalty gap treats a symptom of size.
- Reach broadly within the category, not narrowly at the hand-raisers — the 95% (SKILL.md Rule 4) is where memory is built.
- Brand effects compound past six months; activation effects decay in weeks (Binet & Field). Judging brand spend on a monthly dashboard guarantees it looks wasted (→ SKILL.md Traps).

## Distinctive Assets

Assets are the retrieval handles: color, logo form, character, tagline, sound, packaging shape, spokesperson. Rules:

- Distinctive ≠ differentiated. An asset does not need to mean anything; it needs to be uniquely yours and instantly linked to you.
- Use them identically, everywhere, for years. Every refresh resets accumulated recall — the compounding *is* the asset.
- Test each asset on two axes: **fame** (what share of the category recognizes it) and **uniqueness** (what share attributes it to you rather than to a competitor). High fame + low uniqueness = you are advertising the category leader.
- The most common self-inflicted wound: a new marketing leader or agency changes the asset set to demonstrate impact. Changing assets is a strategy decision with a multi-year cost, never a taste decision.

## Sizing Brand Budget (ESOV)

ESOV = share of voice − share of market. Brands whose SOV exceeds their SOM tend to grow: roughly **+0.5 points of market share per year per 10 points of excess SOV** (Binet & Field, IPA databank). Use it to size ambition against budget, not as a guarantee.

Worked: category ad spend $100M, your share of market 5%, your spend $8M → SOV 8%, ESOV +3 → ~+0.15 pt/year expected. Wanting +1 pt/year implies ESOV ≈ +20, i.e. ~$25M of voice — usually the honest moment to change the ambition or switch to a strategy that does not run on paid reach.

Brand vs. activation split: 60/40 average, B2B ~46/54 (SKILL.md Rule 3). The split is a portfolio decision that sits *across* channels — a search ad can build memory and a brand film can drive signups; classify spend by the job it does, not by the channel it runs in.

## Brand Tracking Without a Six-Figure Study

Ordered by cost:

1. **Share of search** — your brand's search volume ÷ total category brand search volume. Free, weekly, and it tends to lead share of market (Les Binet's share-of-search work). The best single early-warning metric for a startup.
2. **Direct and branded traffic** trend, normalized for paid brand-term spend.
3. **"How did you hear about us?"** free-text on every signup and demo form — the cheapest attribution instrument you will ever install (`measurement.md`).
4. **Win rate against a named competitor** over time — brand shows up as fewer deals lost on "never heard of you".
5. **Prompted/unprompted awareness panel** — worth the cost from roughly Series B, and only if the same instrument runs on the same cadence for years; a one-off wave has nothing to compare itself to.

## Brand Architecture

Monolithic (one brand, all products) is the startup default: every dollar builds one asset. Split into endorsed brands or a house of brands only when a product targets a conflicting audience or price tier that would damage the master brand, or when an acquisition arrives carrying real equity of its own.

Cost of a split: separate awareness budgets, separate assets, and a sales conversation that now needs a company explanation before a product explanation. Sub-brands created for internal-team pride are pure cost.

## Rebrand Decision

Legitimate triggers only: legal necessity, M&A, a name that actively blocks the market (unpronounceable, wrong association, blocked in a target country), or a strategy shift the old brand cannot carry. "Growth is slow" is not on the list — that's positioning or product. A rebrand consumes quarters of team focus and resets distinctive-asset equity to near zero.

If it proceeds: keep the highest-recognition asset if you legally can (usually the colour or the mark), migrate in one dated cutover rather than in waves, and budget the re-education spend inside the project instead of discovering it next quarter.

## Sponsorships, Events, and Brand Partnerships

Sponsorship is reach with borrowed association. Evaluate on: audience overlap with the ICP, whether your distinctive assets are actually visible (a logo in a footer builds nothing), and whether it renews — one-shot sponsorships buy no memory. Price it against the equivalent paid reach and demand a discount for the risk.

Brand safety runs both ways: contract an exit clause for reputational events, and never sponsor anything you would not defend in a press statement (`comms.md`).

## Cutting Brand Spend

When the cut order comes, the sequence that protects the most future revenue: stop producing new assets before you stop distributing proven ones; reduce frequency before reducing reach; keep the one channel where your assets are strongest. Going fully dark is the expensive choice — competitors buy your share of voice at a discount while you are silent, and re-entry costs more than the pause saved (the IPA position; the counter-argument and the deciding variable are in SKILL.md Where Experts Disagree).
