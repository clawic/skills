# Communications — Press, Analysts, Thought Leadership, Crisis

## What Earned Media Can and Cannot Do

Press does not generate pipeline in most B2B categories; it generates credibility, recruiting reach, and a citation buyers find when they check you out. Fund it as brand, judge it on brand metrics (`brand.md`), and stop asking a coverage hit to produce demos.

What actually gets covered: a number nobody else has (original research, `content.md`), a named customer with a real outcome, funding and milestone news, a genuine conflict or contrarian position, and a story where a real person is at risk of something. What does not: a feature release, a partnership without customers, an award you paid to enter.

## Pitching Journalists

- Research the individual, not the outlet: read their last five pieces and pitch the beat they actually cover.
- Subject line is the pitch. Three sentences in the body: what happened, why it matters to their readers, what you can provide (data, an interview, an exclusive).
- Offer an exclusive to one publication or an embargo to several — never both to the same story, and never break an embargo you granted.
- Do not attach a press release as the pitch. Link it.
- Silence is an answer. One follow-up, then move on; persistence buys a blocked address.
- Never ask to review the article before publication. You can offer fact-checking on technical details, which is a different and welcome request.

Press releases exist mainly for the record and for wire distribution requirements (funding, public-company obligations). Judge the wire service by whether you need the disclosure, not by "pickup" counts.

## Analyst Relations

Relevant when enterprise buyers use analyst shortlists. It is a long, structured relationship, not a purchase: brief regularly with real data, respond to inquiries fast, and treat analysts as informed skeptics who talk to your buyers weekly — their questions are a free win/loss study (`positioning.md`).

Paid advisory does not buy placement in an evaluation, and treating it as if it does is how companies waste the budget and the relationship. The value is preparation and feedback; the placement follows the evidence you can show.

## Thought Leadership and Executive Presence

- One executive, one topic, consistently, in public. A rotating cast of ghostwritten posts builds nothing.
- The bar is a position someone could disagree with. Consensus content is invisible.
- Ghostwriting works only when the ideas are the executive's — the interview is the work; the writing is transcription with structure.
- Measure it as brand: inbound speaking and press invitations, sales mentioning it, share of search (`brand.md`). Not impressions.

## Community and Creator Partnerships

Norms punish extraction. Rules that survive: contribute before promoting; disclose the commercial relationship every time (`compliance.md`); pay creators for work rather than trading "exposure"; and give them the freedom to be critical — a partner who cannot say anything negative reads as an ad, and audiences price it that way. Brief on the message, never on the words.

## Crisis Communications

Prepare before you need it: playbook, designated and media-trained spokesperson, stakeholder notification list, holding statements pre-approved by legal, and a decision tree naming who can authorize a public statement out of hours.

Respond in this order:
1. **Acknowledge** — before you have answers; silence fills with speculation.
2. **Empathize** — name the impact on those affected.
3. **Act** — concrete steps with a named owner.
4. **Update on a stated schedule** — "next update by 5pm" beats "we'll keep you posted."

Rules: one voice — every channel routes through one approver; never speculate on cause before facts are confirmed; honesty beats optics — a cover-up converts an incident into a story; document timeline and decisions as you go (legal defense and postmortem both need it).

**Order of audiences**, always: affected customers → all customers → employees → partners and investors → public. An employee or a customer learning from the press is a second incident. In practice employees see everything within minutes, so treat internal comms as public writing.

**Pause automation immediately.** Scheduled social posts, lifecycle sends, and paid campaigns running during an outage or a tragedy are the most avoidable self-inflicted damage in this file. The pause switch should be one person's documented job.

## Severity Ladder

| Signal | Level | Response |
|--------|-------|----------|
| Isolated complaint, no pattern | Monitor | Reply publicly once, resolve privately |
| Repeated complaints on one theme, no press | Elevated | Named owner, holding statement drafted, exec briefed |
| Coverage by a real outlet, or a customer-impacting outage | Crisis | Full playbook, spokesperson live, updates on a schedule |
| Legal, safety, data-breach, or regulatory exposure | Stop | Legal and the CEO lead; marketing does not publish anything before counsel clears it, including apologies (`compliance.md`) |

Any breach of personal data has statutory notification deadlines in many jurisdictions and a legally prescribed audience order — this is the one crisis where communications takes instruction rather than leading.

## Layoffs, Outages, and Other Self-Inflicted News

- Announce internally first, in person or live, then publish. Leaks happen when the gap is measured in days.
- Say what happened, what it means for the people affected, and what you are doing. Do not use growth language to describe a contraction — "rightsizing" costs more credibility than the news itself.
- For outages: a public status page with timestamps, a root-cause write-up when facts are confirmed, and no blame aimed at a vendor or an employee.
- Expect the statement to be screenshotted forever. Write it for the reader who is angry and quoting it, not for the reader who is sympathetic.
