# Positioning and Messaging

## Positioning, in Dunford's Order

Work the steps in this order (April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*) — starting from "what are we?" produces category clichés:

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what customers would actually do without you (often spreadsheets, an intern, or "nothing", not your named competitors).
2. **Unique attributes** — capabilities the alternatives cannot claim.
3. **Value with proof** — what those attributes let customers achieve, evidenced.
4. **Who cares most** — the segment for whom that value is urgent.
5. **Category context** — the frame that makes the value obvious without explanation.

Then compress: For [segment] who [struggle], [product] is the [category] that [value]. Unlike [alternative], we [differentiator].

Tests:
- **Swap test** (SKILL.md Rule 2): put a competitor's logo on your headline — if it still works, there is no positioning.
- **"So what" chain**: every claim must survive two consecutive "so what?"s and land on customer money, time, or risk.
- **Five-second test**: five ICP strangers, five seconds on the homepage — what does it do, who is it for, what does it replace? Two of three wrong = positioning, not copy.
- **The "unlike" test**: if the differentiator after "Unlike" is also true of the top three alternatives, it is a table stake, not a differentiator.

## Segmentation and ICP

Segment by *behavior and trigger*, not by firmographics alone: two 200-person companies buy differently depending on whether they just lost a customer to a compliance failure. The usable ICP has four fields: who they are, what event makes them look, what they use today, what disqualifies them. The disqualifier field is the one teams skip and the one that saves sales capacity.

Rank segments on three axes — urgency of the pain, willingness to pay, and reachability. A segment that scores high on the first two and low on the third is a trap: you will spend the differentiation advantage on finding them.

## Messaging Hierarchy

Brand promise → value proposition → 3-5 message pillars → proof points per pillar.

- Proof points are named numbers and named customers, never adjectives. Every claim needs substantiation attached at the time it ships (`compliance.md`).
- Test messages where money changes hands: A/B in paid ads and live sales calls — not colleague reviews or preference surveys (stated preference diverges from buying behavior).
- Mine call recordings for winning vocabulary; the phrase that closes deals goes into the headline verbatim.
- One idea per pillar. A pillar that requires a sub-clause to be understood is two pillars, and both will be forgotten.

## Message Testing, Cheapest to Most Expensive

| Method | Cost | What it actually measures | Trap |
|--------|------|---------------------------|------|
| Sales-call language mining | Free | Words buyers already use | Confuses the loudest customer with the median one |
| Paid search ad copy test | Low | Click intent against a query | Only tests headline-length claims |
| Paid social creative test | Low-medium | Which problem framing stops the scroll | Creative quality confounds the message |
| Landing-page A/B | Medium | Message → conversion, end to end | Needs traffic volume; see `cro` for sizing |
| Win/loss interviews | Medium | Why the decision actually went that way | Requires a neutral interviewer, never the AE |
| Message-testing surveys | Medium | Comprehension and recall, not purchase | Stated preference; treat as a comprehension check only |

Default: mine calls, then test the two best framings in paid search, then commit. Skip surveys unless the question is "is this understandable", which they answer well.

## Naming and Category

- A name has one job: be sayable, spellable after hearing it once, and clearable (legal clearance before attachment — `compliance.md`). Descriptive names lower the cost of explanation; abstract names raise it and buy room to expand. Below Series A, choose the cheaper-to-explain option.
- Feature and product names inside a portfolio are a tax: every new capitalized noun is a term the market must learn. Name a thing only when customers must ask for it by name.
- **Category creation** is a multi-year, funding-heavy bet: you pay for the education of every buyer in the category and competitors ride it for free. Default to positioning inside an existing category with a sharp differentiator; create a category only when the existing frame actively misprices you (buyers compare you to something 10× cheaper that solves a different job).

## Win/Loss as the Positioning Feedback Loop

Run interviews quarterly on a rolling sample of both outcomes, conducted by someone with no commission in the deal. Four questions carry most of the value: what changed that made you look; what alternatives made the shortlist and why; what almost stopped you; how did you describe us internally to get approval. The last one is the highest-yield sentence in marketing — it is the message your champion actually used, tested against a hostile audience.

Feed the output into positioning quarterly, not continuously; positioning that changes monthly is not positioning.

## Rewriting the Homepage

Order of operations: positioning → hero claim → proof strip → the "unlike" section → path to next step. Rules that survive most redesigns:
- The hero answers what/who/instead-of in one screen without scrolling.
- One primary action per page; a second equal-weight CTA halves the first.
- Social proof adjacent to the claim it proves, not in a logo carousel at the bottom.
- Pricing reachable in one click, even if the number is "talk to us" — hiding it filters out the researchers who become buyers.
- Change one layer at a time when traffic is low; a full-page rewrite with 400 visits/month is untestable, so decide by judgment and say so out loud.
