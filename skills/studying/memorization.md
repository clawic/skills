# Memorization — Fact-Heavy and Verbatim Material

Two distinct tracks: fact-heavy (many small items — vocab, anatomy, dates, statutes) runs on successive relearning; verbatim (exact wording — formulas, quotes, scripts) runs on cue reduction with a stricter criterion.

## Fact Track

- Engine: successive relearning (Rule 3) on flashcards; card design and deck mechanics belong to the `flashcards`/`anki` skills — this file governs load and scheduling.
- Throttle: when today's review queue projects past `daily_review_cap`, add zero new cards; resume at half the previous new-card rate once the queue clears. A drowning queue is a scheduling failure, not a discipline failure.
- Card grain: one retrieval act per card. "List the 12 cranial nerves" is 12 cards plus one ordering card; the mega-card trains partial credit the exam won't give.
- Direction: production beats recognition — test the direction the exam demands. Foreign vocab defaults to L1→L2 (harder now, more durable later); add L2→L1 only for reading-focused exams.
- Interference: confusable pairs (similar drugs, affect/effect, look-alike kanji) — separate their first appearance in time, then add one explicit contrast card ("X vs Y: the difference is …"). Introducing confusables together is how they fuse.

## Mnemonics — Pointers, Not Understanding

Mnemonics store arbitrary mappings; they rate low for durable meaning (Dunlosky) and never replace retrieval reps. Use where the content is genuinely arbitrary:

- First-letter/acronym: short fixed lists. Retire once direct recall works.
- Memory palace (method of loci): ordered lists and speeches. Setup cost is real — worth it for material reused across a course, not for one quiz.
- Keyword mnemonic: vocab with no cognates; drop the image once recall is direct — the image becomes overhead.
- Number systems (major system): only for number-dense domains; learning the system costs more than most courses repay.
- Every mnemonic still enters the spaced schedule. Mnemonic created ≠ item stored.

## Verbatim Track (formulas, quotes, definitions, scripts)

- First-letter reduction: write only each word's first letter, recall the full text from the cues; over sessions degrade the cues (full text → first letters → blank page).
- Criterion is stricter than the fact track: Rule 3 applies but "correct" means flawless — a legal definition that is 90% right can be 100% wrong.
- Formulas: each session pairs one cold recall with one applied use — a formula recalled but never applied fails the transfer question (`problem-subjects.md`).
- Chunk at natural boundaries (clause, line, equation term), not at arbitrary lengths.

## Ordered and Spatial Material

- Sequences (taxonomies, process steps, protocols): test forward, backward, and from the middle ("what follows X?") — exams cue mid-sequence, self-tests rarely do.
- Diagrams, maps, anatomy: redraw on a blank page from memory. Labeling a printed diagram is recognition wearing a lab coat.
- Timelines: test both directions, date→event and event→date; courses ask both.

## What Memorization Cannot Fix

- An item that keeps slipping despite clean reps usually has no structure to hang on: one comprehension pass (why is this list in this order? what generates this formula?) before more reps — then back to the schedule.
- Check the past papers' verbs before sizing this track: "list/name/state" justifies heavy memorization; "explain/evaluate/calculate" means facts are feedstock for `problem-subjects.md` or `essays-and-reading.md`, and pure card time is capped accordingly.
