# Essays, Reading, and Notes — Conceptual Courses

Conceptual courses fail through passive input: reading that feels like studying and notes nobody quizzes. Every workflow here ends in a retrieval artifact.

## Reading a Textbook Chapter (the one pass, Rule 7)

1. Convert headings and bold terms into questions BEFORE reading.
2. Read to answer them; mark (don't copy) anything that answers one.
3. Close the book; answer the questions cold, in writing.
4. Check; misses become the session's question-list entries (Session Protocol 2).

Second reads of the same chapter are banned until the question list is exhausted — the reread urge converts to a question attempt (SKILL.md Traps).

## Lectures and Notes

- Capture sparsely live: structure, claims, examples, and anything the lecturer repeats or flags ("this matters", "classic exam question"). Emphasis is free intel on the exam.
- Process within 24h: turn notes into 2-5 questions per topic. Unprocessed notes decay into a pile nobody rereads; the processing pass is where the notes become study material.
- Cornell layout: cue column = retrieval prompts; the weekly pass covers the notes and answers from cues alone.
- Transcription is the failure mode regardless of medium — recording every word leaves no capacity for selection (the longhand-vs-laptop dispute, SKILL.md Where Experts Disagree).
- Slides provided: annotate them, don't re-copy them; write what the lecturer says ABOUT the slide, which is the part that isn't downloadable.

## Papers and Dense Readings (grad level)

- Pass 1: abstract, figures, conclusion — then write the paper's claim in one sentence from memory.
- Pass 2 (methods detail) only if the seminar or exam demands it.
- Per-paper retrieval item: claim, method one-liner, main criticism. Seminars run on the criticism.

## Essay Exam Prep

- Predict questions: past papers + lecture emphasis + seminar recurrences. Maintain a candidate-question list per course; brainstorming it is a legitimate group activity (`techniques.md`).
- The prep artifact is an outline from memory, timed: thesis, argument blocks with evidence tags (names, dates, quotes, studies), counterargument, so-what. Outlines are cheap enough to spar against every predicted question; full essays are not.
- At least one full timed essay before the exam (SKILL.md, T-1 week) — outlining skill does not automatically become paragraph-under-pressure skill.
- Evidence bank per theme: the quotes, dates, and study names go into the fact track (`memorization.md`) as cards; essays are assembled from retrieved evidence, not from vibes.
- If a marking rubric is published, write to it; graders read with it open.

## In-Exam Essay Execution

Outline-first timing, mark-based allocation, and answering the verb: `test-day.md`.

## Hybrid Subjects (languages, history, law)

- Split the material explicitly into tracks: vocab/grammar/case facts/dates = fact track (`memorization.md`); production (essays, speaking, argumentation) = generation practice with correction.
- Studying a hybrid subject with one method is the classic mismatch — flashcards alone fail the essay; essay practice alone fails the vocab section.
- Language production: writing and speaking attempts with correction beat comprehension input once basics exist; the exam's active/passive split decides the practice ratio.
