# Communication — All-Hands, Memos, Bad News, Press

The CEO's output is decisions and language. A decision the company misunderstands was not made.

## The Sequencing Rule

Order every announcement by who is most damaged by hearing it secondhand:

1. The people directly affected (individually, in advance where legally possible)
2. The exec team and managers, with the script and the FAQ, before they are asked
3. The whole company
4. Customers with a relationship at stake, by a human, same day
5. Investors and board — pre-wired earlier for anything consequential (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: board)
6. Press, if at all

Compress the whole sequence into one day for anything leakable. A layoff, a departure, or an acquisition that takes two days to communicate has already been communicated for you, badly (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: layoffs).

## Writing the Memo

Deliverable format follows `deliverable_style`; the structure does not change.

- Lead with the decision in the first two sentences. Context that precedes the point reads as a build-up to bad news and everyone skips to the end anyway.
- Structure: decision → why now → what changes for you → what does not change → what happens next and when → who to ask.
- One page for a decision, two for a strategy change. If it needs five, the thinking is not finished.
- Name the trade-off you accepted. A memo that presents a hard call as costless tells the smart people on your team you either did not see the cost or are hiding it.
- Delete every hedge that survives a re-read: "we're exploring", "at this time", "a number of". Hedges are what people quote back when they lose trust.
- Write the sentence you would not want screenshotted, then decide whether it is true. If it is true and necessary, keep it and own it. If it is neither, the whole paragraph goes.
- Say the number. "Significant growth" is unreadable; "ARR grew from 4.2M to 6.1M" is a fact people can carry.

## All-Hands

Monthly minimum, live, with the CEO present. Structure that survives contact:

| Block | Minutes | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Where we are | 5 | The same five metrics every month, same definitions (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: metrics) |
| Progress against the three priorities | 10 | Including the one that is behind |
| The decision or theme of the month | 10 | One thing, explained properly |
| Recognition | 5 | Specific work, named people, tied to a priority |
| Q&A | 20+ | Submitted in advance and live |

- Repeat the strategy until you are sick of saying it — that is roughly when the org has heard it once. A message delivered a single time was mentioned, not communicated.
- Answer the submitted-question list including the uncomfortable ones. Skipping one visible question costs more credibility than a bad answer to it.
- "I don't know" plus "here is when I will know" is a complete answer. Inventing an answer to look decisive is how CEOs get caught.
- Never announce a decision at an all-hands that any affected person is hearing for the first time.
- Remote and asynchronous: post the recording and a written summary within 24 hours; the summary is what actually spreads, so write it deliberately rather than auto-transcribing.

## Bad News

Four parts, in this order: what happened · what it means for you · what we are doing about it · what I need from you. Nothing else belongs in the message.

- Deliver it early and complete. Drip-feeding bad news makes every subsequent update suspect, and the team spends the interval speculating instead of working.
- Own the decision in the first person. "The board decided" is true and useless — you were in the room, and passive voice reads as evasion.
- No forced optimism in the same breath. Let it be bad, then be specific about the response.
- Anticipate the three questions the room actually has (usually: am I safe, is the company safe, what changed about my work) and answer them unprompted.
- If you cannot yet say something, say that you cannot and when you will be able to. "I can't discuss that until Thursday" is respected; silence is not.

## Hostile Q&A

- Repeat the question as asked, not as you wish it had been asked. Softening it in the repeat is visible to everyone.
- Answer the question that was asked before the one you prepared for.
- Do not punish the asker, ever, including with tone. The room is calibrating whether questions are safe here, and one flinch closes the channel for a year.
- Take the personal or personnel-specific one offline, on the record, with a commitment to a time.
- When you were wrong, say the wrong decision out loud and what you changed. It costs one uncomfortable minute and buys a year of candor.

## Press and Public

- No off-the-record conversation with a journalist you do not have a track record with. "Off the record" is a norm, not a contract, and the rules differ per outlet.
- Decide what you would be comfortable seeing as the headline, then say only that. Journalists write from the most interesting sentence, not the main one.
- Correct facts, not framing (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: crisis).
- Your employees read everything you publish. A public claim your own team knows is inflated costs you internal credibility first.
- Never comment on a rival, a lawsuit in progress, or an unclosed deal. "We don't comment on that" is a complete answer and creates no story.

## Investor and Board Updates

Monthly, especially when it is hard — the first believers in your next round are the investors who watched you report bad months honestly (format and template → SKILL.md Quick Reference: board).

## Traps

| Trap | Why it fails | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Announcing before the FAQ exists | Managers improvise; six versions of the policy exist by lunch | Script and FAQ to managers first |
| Optimism inflation | Every update sounds good; the team stops listening and stops reporting risk upward | Report the behind item first, every time |
| Delegating the hard message | The team reads absence as cowardice, and the message loses its authority | CEO delivers company-level bad news personally |
| Writing to be quoted, not understood | Slogans that survive the meeting but decide nothing | Plain language, one decision per message |
| Different story to different audiences | Employees, customers, and investors talk to each other | One set of facts; adjust depth, never content |
| Silence during uncertainty | The rumor fills it, and the rumor is worse | Publish the cadence: "update every Friday until this resolves" |
