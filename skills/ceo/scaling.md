# Scaling — What Breaks at Each Headcount Threshold

Companies do not degrade smoothly. They work, then break at a size, then work again once the CEO installs the thing that stage requires. The failure is always the same: installing it a year after the pain, having spent that year believing the problem was individual people.

## The Breakpoints

| Size | What breaks | What to install | CEO's job changes to |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-10 | Nothing yet; the room knows everything | Weekly written priorities, one metric | Doing the work, selling personally |
| 10-25 | Everyone-in-every-decision stops fitting; the CEO becomes the approval queue | Named owners per area, one decision doc, a real 1:1 cadence | Deciding what NOT to decide (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: decisions) |
| 25-50 | Informal communication fails: two people confidently give a customer different answers | First managers (promoted with training, not just title), written strategy, planning cadence | Managing managers, hiring the first execs |
| 50-150 | Nobody knows everyone; new hires stop absorbing culture by osmosis; recruiting quality slips | Levels and comp bands, onboarding, explicit values with examples, a real interview loop | Org design, exec team as a team |
| 150-500 | Dunbar's ceiling: coordination cost dominates; politics appears; local optimization | Formal planning, internal comms function, second-line managers, clear interfaces between teams | Choosing the three things, defending focus |
| 500+ | Strategy travels through too many translations to survive | Written strategy repeated relentlessly, leadership development, succession bench (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: succession) | Capital allocation, leadership of leaders |

Hoffman's blitzscaling stages are 10x jumps — family (<10), tribe (10-99), village (100-999), city (1,000-9,999), nation (10,000+) — each needing a different management model. The thresholds in the table above are finer than that on purpose: the pain arrives well before the next 10x, and it is the pain, not the stage label, that tells you what to install. Plan the install for the next threshold while you are at 60% of it — new structure takes a quarter to work, and installing it during the pain guarantees the pain lasts a quarter longer.

## Signals You Passed a Threshold

- Decisions wait for you when you are on a plane.
- Two teams built the same thing, and both were right to think nobody else was.
- New hires take longer to become productive than the last cohort did.
- Your best people are spending their time coordinating, not producing.
- You hear about problems from customers before you hear them internally.
- Meetings grow attendees without adding decisions.
- People ask "who owns this?" and get three answers.

Any two of these at once means the structure is a stage behind. Diagnose the stage, not the person who dropped the ball.

## Hiring Ahead — By Exactly One Stage

Hire people who have operated at your next stage, not two beyond. The exec who ran a 5,000-person org usually cannot run your 50-person one: they are used to a machine you do not have and will try to build it before there is anything to run.

- The test: ask what they would personally do in their first 60 days here. Answers that begin with hiring a team are a stage-mismatch signal.
- Hire for the next 12-18 months of scale. Beyond that you are paying now for a company that may not exist.
- Growth outpacing the team is normal; the failure is refusing to say so. Every scaling company has people who were great at 20 and are struggling at 100 — that is a role conversation, not a character verdict (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: people).

## Manager Math

- Span 5-8 direct reports for managers of ICs; 4-6 for managers of managers, because coaching a manager costs more per head.
- Layers: every layer adds a translation of strategy and roughly a week to any cross-team decision. Under 150 people, three layers is enough (CEO → manager → IC, with function heads); a fourth before then is a symptom of title inflation.
- Adding a layer is justified by span or complexity, never by giving someone a promotion. The alternative promotion path (senior IC ladder) has to exist before the pressure arrives, or every good engineer becomes a mediocre manager.
- Promoting your best IC into management without training loses you an excellent IC and produces a struggling manager. Budget training and a probation understanding, both directions.

## Process Without Bureaucracy

Add process when the failure has occurred twice and cost something both times. Each new process needs an owner, a written trigger ("when X happens, do Y"), and a review date at which it gets deleted if it is not earning its cost.

- Good process removes decisions from the queue. Bad process adds approvals to it — measure by whether the process makes decisions faster at the edge.
- Kill one process for every two you add, or you will arrive at 200 people with a change-advisory board and no memory of why.
- Onboarding is the highest-return process at every stage above 25: time-to-first-contribution is the metric. Measure it per cohort against your own previous cohort, never a benchmark; unowned, it drifts up quietly, and the first evidence is usually a new hire's third week with nothing shipped.

## Scaling Yourself

The CEO's job description is rewritten at each threshold, and most CEOs keep doing the previous version because they are good at it. Each quarter, name the thing you did last quarter that someone else should now own, hand it over with the context and the authority, and accept it being done at 80% of your standard — at scale, 80% done by an owner beats 100% done by a bottleneck (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: ceo-operating-system).

Personal breaking point comes with the same signals as the org's: your calendar is full of things only you can do because you never made anyone else able to do them. That is not commitment, it is a design error.
