# Competition — Responding Without Losing the Plot

Most competitive damage is self-inflicted: a roadmap bent by a rival's press release, a price cut that resets the market, an org that starts measuring itself against the wrong company.

## Classify the Threat Before Responding

| Event | What it usually means | Default response |
|---|---|---|
| Rival raises a big round | Capital, not product-market fit; expect noise for two quarters | None publicly. Expect their spend in your channels and their recruiters in your inbox |
| Rival ships your feature | Your differentiation was a feature, not a position | Check win rates in 30 days, not the roadmap in 30 minutes |
| Rival undercuts on price | They are buying logos, often below their own cost | Hold price, sharpen value, add a term or volume concession instead (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: pricing) |
| Rival wins a deal you expected | One data point until it is three | Loss review with the buyer, not with your rep |
| Incumbent bundles a free version | The dangerous one: distribution beats features | Move to where the bundle cannot follow (depth, workflow, segment) |
| New entrant counter-positioned against you | They can do something you structurally cannot copy without hurting your business | Name it honestly; incumbents lose here by pretending it is nothing (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: strategy) |
| Rival poaches your staff | Comp gap, or your people have no growth path | Fix the cause; counteroffers buy 6 months and teach the wrong lesson (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: people) |
| FUD about you in deals | You are on their battlecard, which means you are winning some | Arm sales with a factual, unemotional rebuttal; never respond in public |
| Rival shuts down or is acquired | Their customers are in play for roughly one quarter | Named migration offer within two weeks, with a human doing the migration |

Default when unclassified: gather three data points from lost deals before changing anything. A competitor's announcement is marketing; your win rate is data.

## The Price-War Arithmetic

Before matching a discount, compute the volume it demands. Extra unit volume needed to hold gross profit = d / (m − d), where m is gross margin and d is the price cut, both as fractions of price.

- 70% margin, 10% cut → 0.10/0.60 = 16.7% more volume just to break even.
- 70% margin, 20% cut → 0.20/0.50 = 40% more volume.
- 40% margin, 20% cut → 0.20/0.20 = 100% more volume — you must double the business.

Price cuts are also close to irreversible: customers ratchet down easily and up painfully, and public list-price cuts reset the market's expectation permanently. Concede term length, volume commitment, or scope before you concede price.

## Win/Loss That Is Actually Useful

- Interview the buyer, not the seller. Reps report price; buyers report risk, timing, and the internal champion who lost an argument.
- Do it within two weeks of the decision, by someone who was not on the deal. Losses go cold and get retold as price.
- Ask: who else was in it, what were the two finalists' differences, what would have had to be true for us to win, who decided.
- Segment the losses. Losing every deal in one vertical is a positioning problem; losing evenly is a product or price problem.
- The CEO reads loss notes directly at least monthly. Summarized loss data always arrives flattering.

## Competitive Intelligence Without Paranoia

Legitimate and sufficient: their pricing page and changelog history, job postings (the roadmap in plain sight — a "sales engineer, healthcare" posting names their next segment), customer reviews, conference talks, their investors' stated theses, and your own lost deals. Track four things per rival — positioning, price, their best segment, and their weakness — on one page, reviewed quarterly.

Out of bounds and career-ending: misrepresenting yourself to get a demo or documents, hiring someone to bring confidential material, or inducing a breach of their NDA. Also worthless: a 40-tab battlecard nobody reads.

## Choosing Not To Compete

- Concede segments deliberately. Where they are structurally advantaged, losing cheaply beats losing expensively — and publishing an honest "not for you, try them" wins the deals you should win (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: strategy).
- Never let a competitor set your roadmap: a feature is worth building because customers pay for it, not because a rival shipped it. Track how many roadmap items exist only because of a competitor; more than one in five means they are running your product team.
- Measure against your own customers' alternatives, including doing nothing in a spreadsheet — that is the real incumbent in most categories.
- Do not mention competitors by name in your own marketing. It gives them free distribution and reads as insecurity to anyone who had not heard of them.

## The Board Conversation

Boards over-index on competitors because it is the easiest thing to have an opinion about. Come with the data — win rate by segment over three quarters, price realization, pipeline sourced against theirs — and a stated position: where you win, where you concede, and what would change that. A board given no position will supply one, and it will be a feature request.
