# Shutdown — Winding Down Well

Nobody plans this and everyone does it badly the first time. The two decisions that determine whether it goes well are made months apart: deciding early enough to have cash for the wind-down, and paying the right people in the right order.

## Decide While You Still Have Cash

Winding down costs money — final payroll, accrued PTO, severance, legal and accounting fees, contract terminations, lease settlement, tax filings, and the months of administration after the doors close. Reserve for it before it becomes unaffordable; a company that hits zero cash has lost every option except the worst one.

Signals it is time, taken together rather than singly: no falsifiable new thesis and no cash to test one (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: pivot); insiders declining to bridge after saying no twice (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: fundraising); the team's best people gone or going; and a market answer that has been consistent for four quarters.

The honorable version of this decision is returning remaining capital rather than spending it on a direction nobody believes in. Investors remember this precisely, and it is the single strongest reference you can carry into your next company.

## Priority of Payments

Get this order right; it is where personal liability lives.

1. **Payroll, including accrued PTO where the jurisdiction requires it, and payroll taxes.** Unremitted payroll taxes withheld from employees can attach personally to officers and responsible persons (in the US, the trust-fund recovery penalty), and no bankruptcy discharges that. Never fund anything else ahead of them.
2. **Secured creditors** — venture debt, equipment financing, anyone with a lien on assets.
3. **Priority unsecured claims** as defined by the applicable regime (some employee claims, certain taxes).
4. **General unsecured creditors** — vendors, landlord, contractors.
5. **Preferred equity** by preference stack order.
6. **Common equity** — in practice, almost never anything.

Paying a friendly vendor or an insider ahead of this order near insolvency is a preference that can be clawed back and can become a personal claim against directors. Once insolvency is in view, decisions are judged by creditors, so document the reasoning for each one (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance).

## Choosing the Mechanism

| Path | When it fits | Reality |
|---|---|---|
| **Solvent dissolution** | Enough cash to pay everyone and distribute the remainder | Cleanest; board and stockholder approval, filings, notice to creditors, final tax returns |
| **Assignment for the benefit of creditors (ABC)** | Insolvent, some assets, want speed and privacy (US) | Assignee takes control and liquidates; faster and cheaper than bankruptcy, and the common venture-backed route |
| **Chapter 7** | Insolvent, contested claims, need the court's protection | Trustee controls everything; slower, public, more expensive |
| **Chapter 11 / restructuring** | A viable business with an unviable balance sheet | Rarely worth it below substantial scale — the professional fees eat the company |
| **Acquihire / soft landing** | The team is the asset and someone wants it | Preferred outcome; run it while people are still there (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: exit) |
| **Non-US** | Varies widely | Many jurisdictions impose a duty to file for insolvency within a short statutory window once insolvent, with personal liability for directors who trade on. Get local counsel the week you see it coming |

Talk to counsel experienced in wind-downs before choosing. This is the single decision where cheap advice is most expensive.

## The Sequence

1. **Board approves** the wind-down and the plan, with the record of the deliberation. Notify your D&O insurer and confirm run-off coverage — claims arrive after the company is gone (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance).
2. **Model the wind-down budget** to the last dollar: payroll to the end date, severance, PTO payouts, notice-period obligations, legal, accounting, insurance run-off, lease and vendor settlements.
3. **Notice obligations**: WARN and its state equivalents apply to closings, not just layoffs (thresholds → SKILL.md Quick Reference: layoffs). Employment terminations in non-US jurisdictions carry statutory notice you must fund.
4. **Tell the team in person, all at once**, with the date, their final pay, health coverage end, PTO payout, equity reality (state plainly that the options are almost certainly worthless — vague hope costs people money in exercise fees), and what help you will give. Then spend real effort on placing them: intro lists, a shared candidate doc, calls to peer CEOs. This is the part people remember for a decade.
5. **Customers** get as much notice as you can afford, a data export, and where possible a migration path or an introduction to an alternative. Data deletion commitments must actually be honored; obligations survive the company.
6. **Sell the assets**: IP, domain, code, brand, customer contracts (subject to assignment clauses), and hardware. Something is usually worth something, and it goes to creditors.
7. **Investors** hear before the announcement, individually, from you. Include what remains and what you plan to return.
8. **Close it properly**: final tax returns, payroll accounts, state registrations in every jurisdiction where you registered, cancel subscriptions and the registered agent last. An entity left half-dissolved keeps generating franchise tax and penalties for years.

## What Not To Do

- Do not keep going on personal credit cards or founder loans hoping for a miracle; you convert a company failure into a personal one.
- Do not miss payroll to buy two more weeks. It is the one irreversible mistake in this file.
- Do not let people find out by having their access shut off, or from a press piece.
- Do not go silent with investors. The wind-down conversation is uncomfortable for one week; the reputation for disappearing lasts a career.
- Do not promise employees the equity might still be worth something.
- Do not skip the write-up. A short, honest post-mortem — what you believed, what was true, what you would do differently — is the thing that gets you the next round of trust (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: decisions).
