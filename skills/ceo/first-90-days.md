# The First 90 Days in the Seat

Applies to an externally hired CEO, an internally promoted one, and a founder formally taking the role for the first time. The mistake shared by all three is acting on the pattern that made them successful before diagnosing which situation they are actually in.

## Diagnose the Situation First (Watkins, STARS)

| Situation | What it needs | The fatal mismatch |
|---|---|---|
| **Start-up** | Build structure from nothing, hire, choose focus | Running a listening tour when there is nothing to listen to |
| **Turnaround** | Fast, visible, unpopular cuts; centralize | Consensus-building while cash burns (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: crisis) |
| **Accelerated growth** | Scaling systems, hiring ahead, process (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: scaling) | Adding controls that slow the thing that is working |
| **Realignment** | Convince a successful org that it is heading for trouble | Declaring an emergency nobody believes yet |
| **Sustaining success** | Protect what works, find the next S-curve | Changing things to demonstrate you are in charge |

Most companies are a mix — name the dominant one per function; the go-to-market org may be a turnaround while engineering is sustaining success. Your first-90-days plan differs per function accordingly.

## Days 1-30: Learn, Deliberately

- **Listening tour with a fixed question set** so answers are comparable: what should we keep, what should we change, what would you do in my seat, what are we not talking about? Same four questions to 20-40 people across levels and functions.
- Talk to 10 customers and 5 churned ones in the first month. In week one for a turnaround.
- Read before you ask: the last four board decks, last year's plan versus actuals, the org chart with tenure, the top 20 accounts, the last two exec departures' reasons.
- Meet every board member 1:1 and ask each what success looks like at 12 months and what they would have done differently. Divergent answers between directors are the most important thing you will learn this month (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: board).
- Establish the numbers you will run on and their definitions before you inherit someone else's dashboard (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: metrics).
- Say almost nothing definitive in public. Every early opinion becomes policy whether you meant it or not.

Exception: if the company is out of cash or in an active crisis, skip to acting on day one and run the listening tour in parallel. Runway under `runway_alarm_months` overrides the calendar (SKILL.md rule 4).

## Days 31-60: Decide

- Publish your read: what you learned, what stays, what changes, what you are still deciding and when you will decide it. A written memo, not a slide deck (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: communication).
- Name the three priorities and the kill list. The kills are what make the priorities credible (SKILL.md rule 2).
- Establish the operating cadence — weekly exec, monthly all-hands, board rhythm — and hold it exactly. Predictability is the first thing the org is testing.
- Take on the one visible problem everyone has complained about and fix it. An early win is not a symbolic gesture; it is evidence that raising a problem produces action.
- Do not reorg yet unless the situation is a turnaround. A reorg in the first 60 days uses your only credibility on a change you cannot yet justify.

## Days 61-90: Commit

- Publish the plan for the next two quarters with owners, dates, and the metrics that will show progress.
- Complete the exec team read. By day 90 you should know who stays, who needs a different role, and who leaves. Working rule, not a survey finding: the exec you privately doubt in month two is the one you will wish you had moved in month six — act on the doubt rather than waiting for evidence that convinces everyone else (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: people).
- Move on the clearest exec case first, well and generously. The org is watching how you treat someone's exit more than who you exited.
- Rebuild the bench you will need for the plan; do not backfill the org you inherited.
- Formal 90-day review with the board: here is what I found, here is the plan, here is what I need from you.

## If You Were Promoted From Within

- Your peers are now your reports, and the relationship has changed whether you address it or not. Address it explicitly in the first week, one by one.
- The person who wanted the job and did not get it: talk to them in the first 48 hours, be direct about their path, and accept that they may leave. Pretending it did not happen guarantees it goes underground.
- Hand over your old job completely and visibly. Keeping a piece of it tells everyone the new role has not started.
- Your advantage is context; your risk is that you inherit the previous strategy's assumptions unexamined. Run the listening tour anyway — people will tell the new CEO things they never told the peer.

## If You Are a Founder Taking the Title Formally

- The change is real even if the work looks the same: you now have to be legible to a board, a team, and eventually a buyer. Legibility means written strategy, kept cadence, and decisions with owners and dates (SKILL.md rule 9).
- What you should keep doing personally: strategy, top hires, the surface where your judgment is the product. What must be handed over: the queue of approvals you have been absorbing (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: scaling).

## Traps

| Trap | Why it fails | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Big changes before diagnosis | You break the thing that was working and nobody tells you which one it was | 30 days of structured listening (crisis excepted) |
| Importing your old playbook wholesale | It was fitted to a different situation and stage | Diagnose STARS per function, then adapt |
| Bringing three former colleagues in month one | The org reads it as a takeover and stops telling you things | One trusted hire early at most; earn the rest |
| Trashing your predecessor | Signals insecurity and insults everyone who worked for them | Neutral about the past, specific about the future |
| Promising an answer date you then miss | Your first broken commitment sets the standard | Commit to fewer dates and hit all of them |
| Skipping the board 1:1s | You discover the board is not aligned during your first hard decision | Individual 1:1s in the first two weeks (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: board) |
