# Partnerships — Which Ones Are Real

Most partnerships produce a press release and nothing else. The base rate is bad enough that the default answer is no, and the burden of proof sits with the person proposing it.

## The Four Tests Before Any Partnership

1. **Whose number moves?** Name the person at the partner whose compensation depends on this working. No such person, no partnership — you have a logo swap.
2. **Is it their top three?** A partnership that is your priority one and their priority eleven will be staffed accordingly. Ask what it displaces on their roadmap.
3. **What does the first 10k of revenue look like, and by when?** If nobody can describe the first transaction concretely, there will not be one.
4. **What does it cost us if it works?** Success has a price: engineering commitment, margin share, channel conflict with your direct sales, a dependency you cannot exit.

Kill criteria set in advance: a date and a number at which the partnership is wound down without a debate.

## Types and Their Real Failure Modes

| Type | What you get | How it actually fails |
|---|---|---|
| Reseller / channel | Their sales force, their customer relationships | They sell what is easiest to sell; without enablement and margin, you are the third option in the bag |
| Referral | Warm leads at low cost | Nobody's job; volume decays to zero in two quarters unless a person owns it on both sides |
| Technology / integration | Retention lift, joint customers | Built, announced, then unmaintained — their API changes and nobody notices for a month |
| OEM / white label | Volume, deep revenue | You lose the end customer relationship, the brand, and eventually the pricing power |
| Marketplace listing | Distribution, procurement path | Listing is not distribution; ranking and co-selling incentives are, and both cost money |
| Co-marketing | Reach, credibility | Effort symmetric, benefit asymmetric toward the bigger brand |
| Strategic investment from a partner | Capital plus commitment | Signals exclusivity to their rivals, complicates your next round and any sale (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: exit) |

## Terms That Decide the Outcome

- **Exclusivity**: never grant it without a floor — a minimum volume or revenue commitment that, if missed, converts the deal to non-exclusive automatically. Territory or vertical exclusivity is more survivable than product exclusivity. Exclusive means you cannot serve a customer who wants you directly; price that.
- **Term and exit**: 12 months with renewal by mutual agreement beats a 3-year deal with a termination clause you will not want to use. Include what happens to shared customers on exit — that is the clause you will need.
- **Margin and who owns the customer**: who bills, who supports, who holds the data, who renews. Ambiguity here is where partnerships turn adversarial.
- **Enablement obligations**: named people, training dates, deal registration. A partnership without deal registration produces channel conflict with your own reps within a quarter.
- **MFN and change of control**: a most-favored-nation clause constrains your pricing everywhere; a change-of-control clause in a partner contract can complicate or kill your acquisition (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance).
- **Support and SLA pass-through**: if they sell your SLA, you are on the hook for a promise their salesperson made.

## Making One Work

1. One owner on each side with the deal in their goals — CEO-to-CEO enthusiasm does not survive either party's next quarter.
2. Start with a bounded pilot: 90 days, defined scope, one metric, both sides' effort written down.
3. Land one joint customer before building anything general. The first real deal reveals whose commitment is real.
4. Cadence: monthly working call at the operator level, quarterly at exec level. Skipped operator calls are the leading indicator of death.
5. Announce after there is a customer, not before. A launch announcement is the traditional peak of most partnerships.
6. Review at the pre-set date against the pre-set number, and wind down cleanly when it misses. Zombie partnerships consume enablement, support, and roadmap attention forever.

## Channel Conflict

Decide the rules before the first conflict, in writing: which segments or geographies are direct, which are partner-led, how a contested account is resolved, and whether reps are paid on partner-sourced revenue. Reps paid nothing on partner deals will actively sabotage the channel, quietly and effectively.

## The Big-Partner Asymmetry

When the partner is 100x your size: their legal process alone runs months, their champion will change roles mid-deal, and their standard contract will include terms (audit rights, MFN, IP licensing, unlimited liability) that a startup should not sign. Get a second champion before you invest, cap your liability, keep the term short, and never bet the roadmap on their timeline. The reputational lift is real — the revenue usually is not, and it arrives a year after the announcement.
