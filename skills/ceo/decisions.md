# Decision Frameworks

## Speed vs Reversibility

| Two-way door (reversible) | One-way door (irreversible) |
|---------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Decide at ~70% of the information you wish you had (Bezos, 2016 letter) | Slow down; run a pre-mortem; get more data |
| Delegate to a single owner | Escalate to CEO, often to board |
| Wrong call = walk back, learn | Wrong call = permanent damage |
| Pricing test, feature, contractor, process change | Pivot, exec exit, term sheet, layoff, acquisition |

The common failure is misclassification in one direction: mature orgs treat two-way doors as one-way and grind to a halt — that asymmetry, not recklessness, was Bezos's actual warning.

**Tiebreak when unsure on timing**: compare cost of delay to cost of error. If a week of delay costs more than a wrong-but-correctable call, decide now.

## Who Decides What

CEO reserves three classes: one-way doors, cross-functional deadlocks, and precedent-setting values calls. Everything else gets one named owner. Escalations must arrive as options plus a recommendation — an open question escalated is delegation upward.

## Pre-Mortem (Gary Klein)

1. Frame: "It is 12 months later. This decision failed badly."
2. Everyone writes reasons **independently and silently** before any discussion — this step blocks anchoring on the senior voice and is the entire method; skip it and you get polite agreement.
3. Read out, cluster, assign mitigations to the top risks.
4. Decide with the risks priced in.

## Decision Journal

For consequential decisions, record: date and context; options considered; criteria (defined BEFORE evaluating options); the decision and why; what would change your mind; a review date.

On the review date, grade the **process**, not the outcome — good decisions lose sometimes and bad ones win (resulting, Annie Duke). Grading outcomes teaches your team to hide risk.

## RAPID (Bain)

| Role | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **R**ecommend | Builds the proposal |
| **A**gree | Must sign off — real veto |
| **P**erform | Executes |
| **I**nput | Consulted, no veto |
| **D**ecide | One name. Not a committee. |

Failure modes: more than one name in D (no decision), a chain of A's (veto paralysis), and Input-holders who believed they were A's — announce the roles before the debate, not after, or the "betrayed" I's undermine execution.

## Disagree and Commit (Grove/Intel)

1. Everyone states their position with reasons — dissent is mandatory before the call, prohibited after.
2. Decision-maker decides and closes explicitly: "I heard X and Y. We're doing Z. What would change my mind: W."
3. Full team commits; no relitigating in side channels.

The stated change-my-mind condition is what makes commitment honest — without it, dissenters wait for failure instead of watching for W.

## Common CEO Decision Traps

| Trap | Fix |
|------|-----|
| Sunk cost | Evaluate only future costs and returns; past spend is gone either way |
| Confirmation bias | Assign a named person to argue the opposite before you decide |
| Analysis paralysis | Set a decision deadline with a default action if it passes |
| HIPPO (highest-paid person's opinion) | CEO speaks last in the debate, first only in the announcement |
| Quick consensus | On big bets, fast agreement means the debate never happened — force the pre-mortem |
| Recency | Zoom the timeframe out one order of magnitude before deciding |
