# Succession — Handing Over the Seat

Two distinct events get conflated: planning for continuity (which every CEO owes the company from day one) and executing a transition (which is a one-way door). The first is cheap and neglected; the second is expensive and usually late.

## Continuity Planning (do this at every stage)

- A sealed emergency-succession note with the board chair: who runs the company tomorrow if you cannot, what they have authority to decide, and where the credentials and key relationships live. Review annually. This is a governance obligation, not morbidity (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance).
- Every exec has a named backup for their function, tested by having the backup run it during vacations.
- The bus-factor audit quarterly: relationships, credentials, and knowledge held by exactly one person (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: crisis).
- Above ~150 people, a leadership bench is a deliverable with names and development plans, not a feeling that the team is strong.

## Founder or Professional CEO

The operating question is not the label — it is whether the current CEO should hold the seat for the next stage.

- **Keeps the seat while**: their learning rate matches company growth, top talent still joins to work with them, and the board trusts them in a crisis.
- **Hand-off signals**: repeated exec turnover traced back to the founder, the founder managing around their own gaps instead of hiring for them, decisions consistently queuing behind them, boredom in a pure-execution phase, or a stage whose core work (public-company discipline, large-scale operations, a regulated market) they neither enjoy nor intend to learn.
- **Try the middle path first**: a strong COO or president covering the gaps beats a CEO swap. Replacing a founder resets vision, conviction, and often the top team; the switching cost is routinely underestimated by boards who have not done it.
- **Keeper test, both directions** (Hastings/Netflix): would the board fight to keep this CEO? Would the CEO fight to keep each exec? A "no" either way is the real conversation, and having it early is the whole skill.
- Not a demotion, and should not be framed as one: the founder who moves to CTO, chief product officer, or executive chairman with a real scope often does the best work of their career. Framed as a defeat, they leave within a year and take the mythology with them.

## Running the Transition

1. The board owns the process; the CEO can lead it but cannot be the sole decider on their own replacement. Charter a small committee with a timeline.
2. Define the role for the next stage, not the current one: what must be true in three years, and what kind of leader gets it there (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: scaling).
3. Internal versus external, decided explicitly. Internal candidates know the business and cost less time; external candidates bring pattern-matching from the next stage and cost 6-12 months of ramp. If an internal candidate exists, tell them they are a candidate and what the bar is — leaving them to infer it loses them either way.
4. Run a real process even with a favorite. An uncontested internal appointment leaves a legitimacy gap that surfaces in the first hard quarter.
5. Backchannel references beyond the provided list, especially from people who reported to them (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: people).
6. Agree the outgoing CEO's future role, board seat, equity treatment, and public story before the offer goes out — not after, when both parties have new leverage.

## The Handoff Itself

- Overlap of 1-3 months is the useful range. Under a month and context is lost; over a quarter and the organization keeps routing to the old CEO.
- Publish the decision rights on day one of the overlap: from this date, the new CEO decides X, the outgoing CEO decides Y. Ambiguity here creates two power centers and every exec picks one.
- The outgoing CEO makes the introductions — board, top customers, key investors, the exec team — and then gets out of the room.
- Announce internally first, then customers and investors, then public, on the same day (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: communication). Tell the exec team individually the day before.
- The outgoing founder staying on the board: workable when the relationship is genuinely good and the roles are written; corrosive when it is a consolation prize. A founder-director who lobbies executives has effectively taken the job back without the accountability.
- The most common failure is the shadow founder: employees who keep asking the old CEO, and an old CEO who keeps answering. Ending it requires the outgoing CEO to redirect every single time, visibly, for a quarter.

## First-Year Support For The New CEO

- The board's job shifts to support for two quarters: honest feedback early, not a surprise at the first review.
- The new CEO gets to rebuild their team; expect exec turnover in the first year and do not read it as a failure of the transition. Read it as failure only when the departures are the people who were performing (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: first-90-days).
- Set the review checkpoints in advance — 90 days, 6 months, 12 months — with what "on track" means at each. Most CEO transitions that unravel do so because nobody defined success before month nine.

## When The Board Is Removing The CEO

- It is a one-way door and rarely a secret; assume the CEO knows before the vote. Prolonging it damages the company more than the decision does.
- Separate the decision from the exit terms; negotiate severance, equity, board seat, and the public statement in one document with independent counsel on both sides (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance).
- Name the interim before the announcement, always. An announcement without a named leader triggers exec departures within days.
- The public story is agreed and identical from both sides. Every founder-firing where the accounts differ becomes a recruiting and fundraising problem for years.
