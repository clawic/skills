# International — The First Market Outside Home

Expansion fails on the same thing almost every time: it was a growth initiative when it should have been a decision about where the company's next scarce quarter of attention goes.

## Should You Go At All

Prerequisites, all four, before the first hire abroad:

1. **A repeatable motion at home.** Expansion multiplies your go-to-market; multiplying something unrepeatable produces a second unrepeatable thing in a harder timezone.
2. **Pull, not push**: inbound demand, unprompted signups, or customers already using you from that market. Entering without existing pull means paying for demand generation and localization at once.
3. **Runway for the ramp**: expect 12-18 months to first meaningful revenue in a new country, longer for enterprise sales cycles. Budget accordingly and never fund it from the number your home market is expected to hit.
4. **An owner who can be sent.** The single largest determinant of country success is the first leader, and no process compensates for hiring the wrong one.

Choose the market on: existing pull, language and contract-law proximity, buyer similarity, and regulatory cost — in that order. Market size ranks below all four, because the biggest market is where your competitors already are and where your playbook is least likely to transfer.

## Employment: Entity or EOR

| Path | Fits | Cost and friction |
|---|---|---|
| Contractor | One person, exploratory, short | Misclassification risk is highest here and grows with duration and exclusivity (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance) |
| Employer of record | 1-10 people, testing a market, speed | A monthly per-employee fee; live in weeks; some countries and equity arrangements are unsupported |
| Own entity | 10+ people, permanent commitment, local invoicing needed | Months to establish, ongoing accounting, payroll, and statutory filings in-country; hardest to unwind |

Default: EOR for the first hires, entity when headcount or invoicing requirements force it. Reversal is the expensive direction — an entity you no longer need takes months and real money to dissolve.

**Permanent establishment** is the trap that surprises CEOs: a person in-country who negotiates or concludes contracts can create a taxable presence for the whole company, regardless of how you classify them. Ask a tax advisor before the first salesperson starts, not at the first audit.

## What Localization Actually Means

Translation is the cheapest part and the least differentiating.

- **Pricing and currency**: local currency pricing, local payment methods (bank transfer and direct debit dominate in much of Europe; cards do not), and prices set to local willingness to pay rather than converted from home (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: pricing).
- **Tax and invoicing**: VAT/GST registration and thresholds, invoice format requirements, e-invoicing mandates in a growing number of countries. Getting this wrong blocks enterprise procurement from paying you.
- **Contracts**: governing law and jurisdiction, statutory consumer rights, mandatory local terms. Your standard MSA will be rejected by large buyers in many markets.
- **Data**: residency and transfer requirements — where the data lives is a purchase criterion in European and public-sector deals (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance).
- **Support hours**: a European enterprise customer with support that starts at 4pm local time will churn regardless of product quality.
- **Buying process**: procurement norms, security questionnaires, and required certifications differ; SOC 2 may not substitute for the local expectation.

## The Country Lead

- Hire a local operator with a network in your buyer's world, not an expatriate manager from headquarters who will spend a year learning what a local knew on day one.
- Give them real authority — pricing latitude, hiring, and roadmap input — with a clear scope. An in-country leader who must escalate every decision to a timezone eight hours away loses to any local competitor.
- Their first 12-month goal is proving the motion in-market with a handful of reference customers, not a revenue target copied from home and divided by two.
- Do not send your second-best home-market seller as a promotion. Country launches fail on judgment, not effort.

## Running a Multi-Timezone Company

- Define the overlap window and protect it. Two to four hours of shared working time is the minimum for a team that must decide together; below that, split ownership of whole domains rather than splitting one domain across the gap (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: scaling).
- Written by default. Anything decided verbally in one timezone is invisible in the other, and the remote office learns that decisions happen without them.
- Rotate meeting inconvenience. A recurring call that is always painful for the same office tells that office what it is worth.
- Deliberately transmit culture: the new country will develop its own operating norms within months. Visit in person early, and bring one of their people to headquarters (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: communication).
- Comp bands per market, published internally with the logic. Cross-country pay comparisons happen within weeks regardless of policy; the only choice is whether you explained it first (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: people).

## Kill Criteria

Set them before launch: the metric, the threshold, and the date at which you either double down or exit. Exiting a country is a real project — employment termination law abroad is not at-will, entities take months to close, and customers need a transition plan. Budget the exit before you fund the entry.
