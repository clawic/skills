# Strategy and Vision

## The Kernel (Rumelt)

Strategy = diagnosis → guiding policy → coherent actions. Missing any of the three, it is not a strategy.

**Bad-strategy detectors** (Rumelt): fluff (jargon restating the obvious), goals mistaken for strategy ("grow 40%" is an outcome, not a policy), a list of initiatives with no trade-off, dodging the hardest problem in the diagnosis.

**The opposite test** (Roger Martin): state the opposite of your strategy. If the opposite is stupid on its face ("we will serve everyone badly"), you have not made a choice — real strategic choices have sane alternatives someone else is pursuing.

## Where To Play / How To Win (Lafley & Martin)

| Question | What to define |
|----------|----------------|
| Where to play | Markets, segments, geographies, channels — and which you refuse |
| How to win | Differentiation, cost advantage, or a power from the table below |
| Capabilities needed | What you must be great at to sustain the win |
| Management systems | Processes that keep the capabilities real |

**Reverse-engineer instead of debating**: for each strategic option ask "what must be true for this to win?" — then test only the conditions you least believe. Converts opinion fights into testable claims.

## Moats (Helmer, 7 Powers)

| Power | Mechanism | When it becomes available |
|-------|-----------|---------------------------|
| Counter-positioning | Incumbent cannot copy without damaging its own business | The startup power — often the only one at seed |
| Cornered resource | Exclusive access: IP, talent, rights | Any stage, if you actually have it |
| Switching costs | Leaving costs the customer more than staying | After adoption, not before |
| Network economies | Value grows with each user | After critical mass, not before |
| Scale economies | Unit cost falls with volume | Growth stage |
| Branding | Habitual trust commands a premium | Years of consistency |
| Process power | Embedded capability rivals cannot copy quickly | Maturity |

Moats are sequenced: pre-traction, a deck claiming "network effects" is claiming a future moat, not a current one. Name the moat you have today and the one you are building toward.

## Focus and the Kill List

- A priority is real when resources moved to it AND something named died for it. Publish the kill list next to the priorities — teams believe the kills, not the goals.
- **The new-CEO test** (Grove, *Only the Paranoid Survive*): "If the board replaced us tomorrow, what would the new CEO do?" Then walk out the door, walk back in, and do it yourself. This is how Intel exited memories.

## Vision

Stability gradient: vision holds for years, strategy revisits annually, tactics quarterly. A vision that changes with each fundraise deck is positioning, not vision.

Template: "We [action] for [audience] so they can [outcome]" — one sentence, no jargon, repeatable by any employee.

## The One-Page Strategy

If it does not fit on one page, the organization cannot execute it. The page: diagnosis (the one problem that matters) · where we play and where we refuse · how we win · the three priorities · what we are killing · the metrics that show it working · the assumptions this rests on. Everything else is a supporting document.

Test of a real strategy: hand the page to a manager two levels down and ask what they should stop doing. If the page does not answer that, it is a description of the company, not a strategy.

## The Assumption Ledger

Every strategy rests on beliefs that could be false. Write them down as a numbered list with, for each: how you would know it is false, who is watching, and by when. Review it at every quarterly strategy review and mark each assumption confirmed, falsified, or still unknown.

Companies rarely die from a wrong strategy; they die from a strategy whose central assumption was falsified in month four and formally revisited in month twenty. The ledger is the cheapest instrument that exists for shortening that gap (when one falls → SKILL.md Quick Reference: pivot).

## Market Sizing Without Fiction

- Top-down TAM ("1% of a 50B market") is a fundraising artifact, not a planning input. It survives because nobody can disprove it.
- Bottom-up is the only version worth planning on: number of qualifying accounts × realistic annual contract value × plausible penetration. It usually produces a number an order of magnitude smaller, and the honest smaller number is what tells you whether the business can carry your funding.
- Segment before sizing. Your real near-term market is the segment where you win today, and that is what determines this year's plan.
- A market too small for the capital you raised is a strategy problem to name in the boardroom, not a marketing problem to spend past (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: board).

## The Second Bet

Every S-curve flattens; the second bet has to be planted while the first is still growing, because that is the only time you can afford it.

- Timing: start when the core business is compounding predictably and the CEO is no longer the bottleneck in it — typically Series B onward (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: scaling).
- Fund it as a separate line with its own metrics, its own small team, and a named owner. Judged against the core's numbers it will always lose, and it will be quietly starved.
- Kill criteria and a date, set at the start, or it becomes a permanent internal science project.
- Two is the maximum. A company running three new bets has no core, only a portfolio it cannot staff.

## Build, Buy, or Partner

Ask in this order: is this the thing customers pay us for? If yes, build — never outsource your differentiation. If it is necessary but not differentiating, buy it if a credible product exists and integration is cheap, or partner if the partner has a real incentive (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: partnerships). Buying a company for it is a different decision with a different failure mode (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: acquisitions), and the honest test for "build" is whether you would prioritize it in the next two quarters — most build cases are really "we would never get to it."

## Annual Planning Rhythm

| When | Activity |
|------|----------|
| Q3 | Strategy review, multi-year direction |
| Oct | Bottom-up team inputs |
| Nov | Leadership alignment, trade-offs made explicit |
| Dec | Board approval, company communication |
| Q1 | Execute, measure, adjust |

Months assume a January fiscal year; shift the whole rhythm by `fiscal_year_start`, keeping the intervals — strategy review one quarter out, board approval one month before the year starts. The planning artifacts themselves follow `planning_framework`.

## Quarterly Strategy Review

1. Which assumption in the plan has been falsified since last quarter (from the ledger above)? If none, you are not measuring assumptions.
2. Which bets get more, which get killed? Every review reallocates something or it was a status meeting.
3. Where is a competitor counter-positioned against us — doing something we structurally cannot copy (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: competition)?
4. What are customers hiring instead of us (Christensen, jobs to be done) — including "doing nothing"?
5. Adjust or stay? Default: stay. Strategy that changes more than once a year is thrash wearing agility's clothes.
