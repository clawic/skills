# Strategy and Vision

## The Kernel (Rumelt)

Strategy = diagnosis → guiding policy → coherent actions. Missing any of the three, it is not a strategy.

**Bad-strategy detectors** (Rumelt): fluff (jargon restating the obvious), goals mistaken for strategy ("grow 40%" is an outcome, not a policy), a list of initiatives with no trade-off, dodging the hardest problem in the diagnosis.

**The opposite test** (Roger Martin): state the opposite of your strategy. If the opposite is stupid on its face ("we will serve everyone badly"), you have not made a choice — real strategic choices have sane alternatives someone else is pursuing.

## Where To Play / How To Win (Lafley & Martin)

| Question | What to define |
|----------|----------------|
| Where to play | Markets, segments, geographies, channels — and which you refuse |
| How to win | Differentiation, cost advantage, or a power from the table below |
| Capabilities needed | What you must be great at to sustain the win |
| Management systems | Processes that keep the capabilities real |

**Reverse-engineer instead of debating**: for each strategic option ask "what must be true for this to win?" — then test only the conditions you least believe. Converts opinion fights into testable claims.

## Moats (Helmer, 7 Powers)

| Power | Mechanism | When it becomes available |
|-------|-----------|---------------------------|
| Counter-positioning | Incumbent cannot copy without damaging its own business | The startup power — often the only one at seed |
| Cornered resource | Exclusive access: IP, talent, rights | Any stage, if you actually have it |
| Switching costs | Leaving costs the customer more than staying | After adoption, not before |
| Network economies | Value grows with each user | After critical mass, not before |
| Scale economies | Unit cost falls with volume | Growth stage |
| Branding | Habitual trust commands a premium | Years of consistency |
| Process power | Embedded capability rivals cannot copy quickly | Maturity |

Moats are sequenced: pre-traction, a deck claiming "network effects" is claiming a future moat, not a current one. Name the moat you have today and the one you are building toward.

## Focus and the Kill List

- A priority is real when resources moved to it AND something named died for it. Publish the kill list next to the priorities — teams believe the kills, not the goals.
- **The new-CEO test** (Grove, *Only the Paranoid Survive*): "If the board replaced us tomorrow, what would the new CEO do?" Then walk out the door, walk back in, and do it yourself. This is how Intel exited memories.

## Vision

Stability gradient: vision holds for years, strategy revisits annually, tactics quarterly. A vision that changes with each fundraise deck is positioning, not vision.

Template: "We [action] for [audience] so they can [outcome]" — one sentence, no jargon, repeatable by any employee.

## Annual Planning Rhythm

| When | Activity |
|------|----------|
| Q3 | Strategy review, multi-year direction |
| Oct | Bottom-up team inputs |
| Nov | Leadership alignment, trade-offs made explicit |
| Dec | Board approval, company communication |
| Q1 | Execute, measure, adjust |

## Quarterly Strategy Review

1. Which assumption in the plan has been falsified since last quarter? (If none, you are not measuring assumptions.)
2. Which bets get more, which get killed? Every review reallocates something or it was a status meeting.
3. Where is a competitor counter-positioned against us — doing something we structurally cannot copy?
4. What are customers hiring instead of us (Christensen, jobs to be done) — including "doing nothing"?
5. Adjust or stay? Default: stay. Strategy that changes more than once a year is thrash wearing agility's clothes.
