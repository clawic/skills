# Layoffs — Sizing, Selection, Notice, and the Day

The decision is arithmetic; the execution is choreography. Both get done badly by CEOs who have never run one, and the cost of doing it twice is measured in the people who stay.

## Sizing (do this before anything else)

Target runway after the cut = months to the next fundable milestone + 6, computed on the bear case: revenue flat, no new round, no unsigned deal counted (SKILL.md rule 5).

Worked example: 900k cash, 150k/month net burn (6 months), milestone 9 months out. Required runway 15 months → required burn ≤ 60k/month → 90k/month must come out. Payroll is typically 60-80% of a startup's burn, so non-payroll levers alone rarely reach it: pull vendor, cloud, marketing, and contractor spend first, then size headcount against the remainder. Assume severance and unused-PTO payouts consume 1-2 months of the savings up front; the cut must be big enough to survive its own cost.

Three sizes exist: too small (you will be back within 9 months, and everyone knows it), right (the plan closes with margin), and too deep (you cannot execute the plan you just kept). Test for too deep: name the person who now does each critical function. If a name repeats three times, you cut into the plan.

## Selection

Select by role and plan, never by tenure default, and never by manager popularity. Sequence:

1. Redesign the org for the post-cut plan on a blank page: which functions exist, at what size, to hit which milestone.
2. Map current people to that org. The gap is the cut. Doing it in this order is what makes the list defensible.
3. Apply consistent criteria in writing: criticality of role to the plan, performance record, skill coverage. Document the criteria before names.
4. Have counsel review the list for disparate impact by age, gender, race, disability, leave status, and recent protected activity. A list that unintentionally clusters on a protected class is a lawsuit even when every individual decision was sound.
5. Keep the people whose absence would break the plan, not the people you enjoy most. Then check the inverse: an underperformer you were going to manage out belongs on this list, but a cut is not a substitute for a performance conversation you owed someone.

Cut managers proportionally. A layoff that only removes ICs leaves a supervision layer with nobody to supervise, and the survivors count.

## Legal Clocks (verify against current counsel; these are the usual traps)

| Regime | Trigger | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| US federal WARN | Employer with 100+ employees; 50+ affected at a single site, or a plant closing | 60 days' advance written notice, or pay in lieu |
| US state mini-WARN | Lower thresholds than federal (California starts at 75 employees; New York and New Jersey have their own) | Notice periods and definitions differ by state — check every state you employ in |
| Older-worker waivers (US, OWBPA) | Asking employees 40+ to release age claims | 21 days to consider (45 in a group program), 7 days to revoke, plus a disclosure of ages and job titles in the decisional unit |
| UK collective consultation | 20+ redundancies at one establishment in 90 days | Consultation starting 30 days before the first dismissal (45 days at 100+), with elected representatives |
| EU works councils | Varies by country | Information and consultation before the decision is announced — announcing first can invalidate the process |

Non-US employment is not at-will. In much of Europe and Latin America a reduction is a negotiated process with statutory notice and payments; assume weeks-to-months, not days, and budget accordingly.

## Severance

Convention, not law (outside jurisdictions that mandate it): a floor of 4-8 weeks of base pay for ICs, plus roughly 1-2 weeks per year of service; 3-6 months for executives, usually written into their offer already. Common additions: health coverage continuation for the severance period, laptop, accelerated vesting or an extended option-exercise window (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance), and reference commitments.

Be more generous than the minimum by whatever you can afford. The delta is small against total burn, and it buys the two things the cut costs you: the departing employees' willingness to speak well of you, and the survivors' belief that this company handles people decently.

Severance is paid in exchange for a signed release. Do not hand out the money and ask for the release later, and do not use the release to buy silence about unlawful conduct — a non-disparagement clause that reaches whistleblowing or harassment is unenforceable in a growing list of jurisdictions and reads as evidence of something worse.

## The Day

Choose the shape deliberately: a single all-hands announcement followed by individual conversations within the hour is the standard for a company under ~200. Larger, or multi-country, and you brief managers the night before with a script.

- Everyone learns the same day. A cut that leaks into a second day converts a hard morning into a week of paralysis.
- Individual conversations first for those leaving, then the all-hands for those staying — never the reverse, and never a group call for the departing.
- The conversation is 5-10 minutes: this is a decision, not a discussion; here is what happens to your pay, health coverage, and equity; here is who answers your questions and when the paperwork arrives. Do not explain the org theory to a person who just lost their job.
- The CEO is in the room. Delegating the announcement to HR is the single most-remembered failure of every badly run layoff.
- Access shutoff is timed to the conversation, not before it. Learning you are cut from a locked laptop is a story that outlives the company.
- Have the FAQ written before the day: severance details, health coverage, equity and exercise deadlines, references, immigration status for visa holders (a 60-day grace period is common in the US and is the most urgent question in the room), final paycheck timing, and what happens to their work.

## Survivors

The layoff ends for the leavers on the day. For the people who stay it starts.

- Say the number and the plan: how many left, why this size, what the remaining team is expected to hit, and — the question everyone is holding — whether another cut is coming. Answer that one honestly, including "I cannot promise, and here is the number I am watching" (SKILL.md rule 4).
- Expect a productivity trough of weeks and voluntary attrition afterwards, weighted toward the people with the most options. Talk to your top performers individually within 48 hours, not with a retention bonus but with a role and a reason.
- Explicitly reassign the orphaned work. Anything not reassigned is quietly absorbed by the most conscientious person on the team, who then leaves.
- Kill projects, not just people. If the workload stays the same with fewer people, you did a burn exercise, not a strategy change.
- Do not backfill within two quarters. Hiring in the function you just cut tells the team the cut was arbitrary.

## Traps

| Trap | Why it fails | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Two cuts in six months | Trust collapses; the best people leave between them | Size on the bear case once (SKILL.md rule 5) |
| Framing it as performance | Departing people hear a lie, survivors hear the criteria wrong | Say it is a plan change and that these roles are not in the new plan |
| Announcing before counsel reviewed the list | Disparate-impact exposure, blown notice periods, invalid releases | Legal review before any name is spoken aloud |
| Leaking to press before employees | Employees learn from a headline; you never recover the narrative | Sequence: individuals, all-hands, customers, investors, press — same day (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: communication) |
| Quiet rolling cuts to avoid a headline | Months of dread, the whole company job-hunting | One day, one number, one message |
| Cutting recruiting and keeping the hiring plan | The plan quietly becomes fiction | Rewrite the plan first; headcount follows the plan |
