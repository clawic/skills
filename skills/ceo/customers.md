# Customers — Where the CEO Belongs in the Account

The CEO is a scarce, high-signal resource. Spend it where a peer-level relationship changes the outcome, and nowhere else — a CEO in every deal teaches the buyer that no deal is real without one.

## When The CEO Shows Up

| Situation | CEO's role | Not the CEO's role |
|---|---|---|
| Deal above `concentration_threshold` (default 10% of revenue) | Peer relationship with their exec sponsor, commitment on roadmap and support | Running the sales process |
| Strategic first customer in a new segment | Design partner, personally on the calls | Support escalation queue |
| Churn signal in a top-10 account | The call within 48 hours, asking the hard question | Discount authority handed out on the call |
| Escalation that has already failed twice | Own it, name the fix and the date, follow up personally | Debugging it live |
| Renewal of the reference customer | Ask for the renewal directly, and for the reference | Negotiating line items |
| Anything else | Stay out; back the account owner publicly | Bypassing your own team to look responsive |

Rule: when the CEO enters an account, the account owner stays in every conversation. A CEO who becomes the customer's contact has just removed their own team's authority, and the account never goes back.

## Customer Contact Cadence

- Pre-PMF: five customer conversations a week, run by a founder, no exceptions and no delegation. This is the job, not research.
- Post-PMF: the CEO talks to at least one customer a week and one churned customer a month. The churned ones are the only honest source of what is actually broken.
- Concentration review quarterly: revenue by account, top-5 share, and which accounts renew in the next two quarters.
- Read raw support tickets or sales-call recordings monthly. Summaries arrive filtered by whoever is being evaluated on them (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: metrics).

## Concentration Risk

One customer above `concentration_threshold` (default 10% of revenue) is a board topic; above 2× it — 20% at the default — it is the company's largest single risk and it belongs on the risk page of every board deck. Consequences worth naming before they arrive:

- Their procurement dictates your terms, your roadmap, and eventually your pricing.
- Acquirers and lenders discount valuation for it, sometimes with an explicit haircut, and diligence will find it (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: exit).
- Losing them is not a bad quarter; it is a restructuring event (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: crisis).

Mitigation is boring and slow: a named diversification target with a date, multi-year contracts with the big account, and multi-threaded relationships (at least three people, not just the champion who could leave next month).

## Churn Saves

1. Get the truth from the economic buyer within 48 hours. Champions soften and reps report price; the buyer reports the real reason, which is usually value never realized, a champion who left, or a reorganization.
2. Ask directly: what would have had to be true for you to renew? The answer is either buyable or it is not, and you find out in one question.
3. Decide save-or-release deliberately. A save that costs a roadmap quarter plus a 40% discount is a loss with extra steps, and worse, it is now the precedent in your renewal book.
4. Offer a genuine fix with a date and an owner, not a discount. Discount-only saves churn on the next cycle at the lower price.
5. Whatever happens, run the exit well: clean data export, no hostage tactics. Churned customers return more often than anyone expects, and they talk to your prospects.

## Firing a Customer

Real and sometimes necessary. The candidates: support cost far above their revenue, roadmap distortion for a segment you are exiting, abuse of your staff, or contract terms you cannot repeat and cannot keep.

Do it with a long runway — a renewal not offered, 90+ days of notice, help migrating, and a written record. Never with a price increase designed to make them leave: they may accept it, and now you are locked in with a resentful customer at a number you have to defend to everyone else.

## Advisory Boards and Design Partners

- A customer advisory board of 8-12 works when you bring real decisions to it; it dies when it becomes a product roadshow. Meet twice a year, share something not yet public, and act visibly on at least one thing they said.
- Design partners get early access and influence in exchange for engagement and a reference — write both sides down. A design partner who pays nothing and commits nothing is a friendly user.
- Never let a single design partner's requirements become the product. Build what three of them independently asked for; the rest is custom work you are not being paid for (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: pivot).

## Traps

| Trap | Why it fails | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| CEO becomes the escalation path | Every account learns to skip your team; the org stops owning outcomes | Enter with the owner, exit with the owner |
| Promising roadmap in a sales call | Engineering learns of the commitment from the customer | "I'll answer that by Friday" — then check with the owner |
| Reading only the wins | Post-mortem your losses and churns instead (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: competition) | Monthly loss and churn review, raw |
| Treating one loud account as the market | Roadmap bends toward whoever escalates hardest | Weight by cohort and revenue concentration |
| Letting a big deal rewrite the contract | One customer's terms become the floor for the next audit or acquirer | Legal red lines set in advance (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance) |
