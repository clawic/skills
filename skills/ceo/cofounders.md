# Co-Founders — Equity, Roles, Conflict, Departure

Co-founder conflict is one of the most common ways early companies die, and it almost always traces back to three conversations that were never had: who decides, who owns what, and what happens if one of us leaves.

## Equity Splits

- Split for the future, not the past. Equity pays for the next five years of work and risk; the person who had the idea is being compensated for a month.
- Near-equal splits are the norm among successful teams and the argument you have about the last 5% is a preview of every future argument. A 60/40 that both parties genuinely accept beats a 50/50 that one resents.
- If a split has to be unequal, write the reason in one sentence you would both read aloud at a board meeting. Reasons that survive: full-time versus part-time, capital contributed, a genuinely irreplaceable asset. Reasons that do not: age, who spoke first, who is louder.
- **Dynamic splits are for pre-formation only.** Once real work starts, an unresolved split poisons every decision and blocks any priced round.
- Do not leave equity unallocated "to decide later". Later is when you have the least leverage and the most emotion.

## The Mechanics You Cannot Skip

| Instrument | Why it exists | The failure it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| Vesting on founder shares, 4 years with a 1-year cliff | Equity is earned over time | A co-founder leaves at month 7 holding 40% forever — the "dead equity" that kills your next round |
| 83(b) election, filed within 30 days of the grant (US) | Taxes the shares at grant value | A tax bill on vesting appreciation with no cash and no way to fix it — the 30-day window has no exception |
| IP assignment from every founder | The company owns what you built | Code written before incorporation belonging to someone personally, or to their old employer |
| Written role and decision split | Names one decider per domain | Two people deciding everything, so nothing gets decided fast |
| Acceleration terms defined at the start | Sets what a change of control does | Discovering in an acquisition that a departed founder's terms block the deal (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: exit) |

If founder shares were issued without vesting, fix it now by agreement while everyone is friendly. Investors will require it anyway, and the conversation is much worse when there is money on the table.

## Roles and Who Decides

- One CEO. Co-CEOs work in a handful of famous cases and fail quietly in most others: employees route to whoever gives the answer they want, and the ambiguity becomes visible to the board at exactly the wrong moment.
- Split by domain, not by seniority, and write it down: each domain has one owner whose decision stands even when the other disagrees.
- Define the small set of decisions requiring both: fundraising terms, hiring or firing an exec, changing the strategy, spending above a threshold. Everything else has one owner.
- Deadlock rule agreed in advance: the CEO decides, or a named board member breaks the tie. Agreeing this while calm is the entire point.
- Non-CEO founders need a real job with real outcomes. "Founder" is not a role; a founder without a domain becomes a floating opinion, which is corrosive to every exec you hire (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: people).

## Maintenance

- Weekly 1:1 between founders, protected, agenda-free enough for the hard thing to surface. Most conflicts are six months of unsaid small things.
- Quarterly relationship review, separate from any business review: what is working, what is not, whether the role split still matches the company's stage.
- Never disagree in front of the team about a decision already made — the org learns which founder to lobby, and you get factions (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: decisions).
- Never let one founder be the only one talking to the board. A board that only hears one founder cannot help with the founder problem when it arrives.
- Say the resentment early, in the 1:1, when it is still a small correction. The version at month eighteen comes with a lawyer.

## When It Is Not Working

Diagnose which one it is, because the treatments are different:

| Pattern | Underlying issue | Move |
|---|---|---|
| Chronic disagreement on direction | Different companies in their heads | Write both strategies down, decide once, formally (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: strategy) |
| One is not delivering | Capability or effort mismatch | Same conversation you would have with any exec, held earlier than feels comfortable |
| One has outgrown the role | The company scaled past them | Role change with dignity while equity stays earned (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: scaling) |
| Trust is gone | Broken commitments or hidden information | Usually terminal; get to a clean separation quickly |
| Both are miserable but neither will say it | Sunk cost | Third party — board member, coach — in the room |

Mediation with an experienced outside party is worth trying once, early. It fails when the real answer is already known and one side is avoiding saying it.

## Separation

1. Decide it properly and fast. A slow founder divorce consumes the whole exec team's attention and every employee can see it.
2. Vested equity is earned and stays. Unvested does not vest as a kindness — it becomes dead equity that your next investor will make you clean up, and you will not be able to.
3. Agree the terms in one document: equity treatment, board seat, title, information rights, severance, non-disparagement, and who says what. Independent counsel for both sides is cheaper than the alternative.
4. Board seat handling matters more than the title: a departed founder holding a board seat can block financings for years (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance).
5. Tell the team promptly with a clean, non-blaming story you can both repeat, and tell investors before they hear it (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: communication). The story you agree on is the one that travels for the next decade.
6. Expect the remaining founder's workload and loneliness to spike. Line up a peer group or coach before the departure, not after (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: ceo-operating-system).
