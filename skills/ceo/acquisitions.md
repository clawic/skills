# Acquisitions — Buying a Company

Most small acquisitions fail on integration, not on price. Decide what you are buying before you decide what it is worth, because the answer determines everything downstream — the price, the structure, and whether the thing you bought still exists in a year.

## What Are You Actually Buying

| Type | You are buying | Price logic | The thing that kills it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acquihire | A team that already works together | Per-engineer convention plus retention packages; verify against current market comps, they move fast | The team leaves at the end of the retention period; you bought a year |
| Product / tuck-in | Shipped code and a roadmap shortcut | Build-cost avoided, discounted for integration reality | Rewriting it anyway within 18 months |
| Customers / book of business | Revenue and logos | Multiple of the revenue that survives the transition | Churn at renewal when their champion sees a new owner |
| Technology / IP | A capability you cannot build in time | Time-to-market value | The one engineer who understands it leaves |
| Competitor | Removing price pressure, consolidating share | Synergy, market structure | Two overlapping products, neither retired, both maintained |
| Distribution | Their channel or market access | Contribution margin the channel can carry | The channel was the founder's relationships |

Write the answer in one sentence before opening negotiations. "We are buying eight engineers who ship in our stack" and "we are buying a product line" lead to completely different deals with the same company.

## Before The Offer

- Build-buy-partner honestly: what would this cost to build, in months, with the team you actually have and their current commitments? Most buy cases are really "we would never prioritize it", which is a valid reason, stated honestly.
- Talk to their customers before you talk about price. Whether the revenue survives an ownership change is knowable in five calls.
- Understand why they are selling. Founders exhausted, out of runway, or facing a competitor's move each imply a different price and a different post-close reality.
- Model the fully loaded cost: purchase price + retention packages + integration engineering + the quarter of distraction across your own team. Integration is routinely under-budgeted because nobody owns it: staff it as a real project with a named owner, a dated end, and engineering capacity carved out of the roadmap before you sign, not after.

## Diligence That Matters at This Size

- **Cap table and clean title**: every share, option, SAFE, note, and side letter accounted for. IP assigned from every founder, employee, and contractor — an unassigned contractor contribution is the most common deal-blocker in small acquisitions (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance).
- **Code and license audit**: open-source licenses (copyleft in a commercial product is a real problem), third-party dependencies, and whether anything was built for a client who owns it.
- **Customer contracts**: change-of-control clauses, assignment rights, termination-on-acquisition, MFN and price commitments you inherit.
- **Employment**: who has an offer letter versus a contractor agreement, non-competes, visa status of key people, accrued and unpaid comp.
- **Liabilities**: unpaid taxes (payroll and sales tax nexus), pending disputes, deferred revenue you must deliver against.
- **The key-person question**: which two or three people must stay for this to work? Their retention terms are part of the deal, not an HR detail after it.

## Structure

- **Price is a package**: cash at close, stock, retention pool, escrow or holdback, earnout. Small-deal convention holds 10-15% in escrow for 12-18 months against reps and warranties; negotiate the cap and survival period, not just the amount.
- **Earnouts**: assume the earnout does not pay. Price the deal on the cash at close, and treat the earnout as an alignment device with metrics that survive your own integration decisions. An earnout tied to a metric you will change is a lawsuit with a schedule.
- **Retention**: separate purchase consideration from retention comp explicitly, vesting over 2-3 years. Sellers will push for value at close; the whole point is what happens after.
- **Deal structure has tax consequences for the sellers** that materially change the price they need (asset versus stock purchase, cash versus stock). Bring tax counsel in before you name a number, or you will renegotiate.

## Integration — Decide Before Signing

The three questions to answer in writing before close, because after close everyone has an opinion:

1. **Product**: sunset, maintain, or merge — with a date. "We'll decide later" means maintaining two products forever.
2. **People**: who reports to whom on day one, which of their leaders keeps a leadership role, and where the team physically and organizationally sits.
3. **Brand and customers**: what the customers of the acquired product are told, by whom, in the first 48 hours.

- Name one integration owner from your side, full-time, senior, for at least a quarter. Part-time integration ownership is why acquisitions drift.
- Day-one basics: systems access, payroll, benefits, email, and a clear statement about what is not changing. Anxiety in the first week produces the departures you spent the retention pool to prevent.
- Compensation and level mapping will be discovered by the acquired team within days. Decide it deliberately and explain the logic, or you import a permanent grievance.
- Kill the acquired roadmap explicitly if you are killing it. The team keeping faith with a dead roadmap is the most common quiet failure.

## Traps

| Trap | Why it fails | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Buying revenue you cannot retain | Renewals arrive and the customers were the founder's relationships | Model on post-transition revenue, structure with an earnout tied to retention |
| No integration owner | Everyone assumes someone else is doing it; six months pass | One named full-time owner before signing |
| Culture "we'll figure out later" | Two operating systems in one company; the acquired team leaves in year two | Decide the operating model pre-close and say it plainly |
| Announcing before the team is told | Their employees learn from press; retention collapses on day one | Their team hears first (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: communication) |
| CEO runs the deal alone | Diligence gaps, and the whole exec team is a spectator to something they must execute | Assign functional diligence owners, keep the board informed early (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: board) |
| Acquiring to fix a strategy problem | You now have two unclear strategies and one integration project | Fix the strategy first (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: strategy) |
