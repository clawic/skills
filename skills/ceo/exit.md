# Exit — When Someone Wants To Buy You

Companies are bought, not sold — but the ones that get bought well were ready, had a process, and had an alternative. The inbound email is the start of a negotiation whether you treat it as one or not.

## The Inbound Approach

An unsolicited approach means one of three things: a real acquisition interest, a competitive intelligence exercise, or a junior corp-dev associate filling a pipeline quota. Distinguish before spending a week on it.

- Ask three questions on the first call: what triggered this now, who internally is sponsoring it, and what does their process and timeline look like? Vague answers to all three mean it is information gathering — share nothing beyond what is public.
- Never send the data room to establish credibility. Metrics you send to a competitor's corp-dev team do not come back.
- Do not name a price first. "What range would make this worth your board's time?" puts the number on their side of the table, where it belongs.
- Tell your board immediately, even for an approach you intend to decline. A board that learns later that you took M&A calls alone will never fully trust you again — and depending on your fiduciary duties, the meeting itself may need to be recorded (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance).

## Deciding Whether To Run A Process

Sell when: the strategic buyer's price exceeds your standalone plan's risk-adjusted value; the category is consolidating and being third is fatal; the team is out of energy for another five years; or the capital required for the next stage is not available to you.

Do not sell because the quarter was bad, because fundraising is unpleasant, or because a single number sounds large before the waterfall (below).

Two structural realities to internalize before deciding:
- A single-bidder process is not a negotiation. Either create a real alternative (other bidders, or a credible plan to stay independent) or accept that the price is theirs to set.
- Your preference stack decides what "a good price" is. Run the waterfall before you form an opinion about a number.

## The Waterfall — Run This First

Founders routinely misjudge an offer by an order of magnitude because they multiply their ownership percentage by the headline price.

Order of payment at close: transaction fees (bankers, legal) → debt → liquidation preferences in stack order (last money usually first) → participation, if the preferred participates → then common, pro rata → minus escrow and holdback amounts released later → minus the retention pool carved for employees, which usually comes off the top.

Worked example: 50M offer, 30M raised across rounds at 1x non-participating preference, 15% retention pool off the top, 10% escrow for 18 months. Sellers' pool at close: 50 − 7.5 (retention) = 42.5; preferred takes 30 unless converting yields more; common splits ~12.5, of which 10% sits in escrow. A founder with 20% of common sees roughly 2.25M at close, not 10M. Run this arithmetic before your first reaction to any number, and re-run it for each structure they propose (how the preferences got there → SKILL.md Quick Reference: fundraising).

## Process Mechanics

| Phase | Duration | What decides the outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation | 4-8 weeks | Clean cap table, IP assignment, contracts, financials that tie out (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: governance) |
| Outreach | 3-6 weeks | Multiple parties contacted in the same window — sequencing is what creates competition |
| Indications of interest | 2-4 weeks | Non-binding ranges; use them to shortlist, not to celebrate |
| Diligence and LOI | 4-8 weeks | Exclusivity starts; your leverage peaks the moment before you sign it |
| Confirmatory diligence and close | 4-12 weeks | Where price gets re-cut; surprises found here are always your fault in their telling |

Total 4-9 months, running in parallel with your day job. Assume the CEO loses 50% of their capacity for the duration, and staff accordingly — the classic disaster is a missed quarter during diligence, which hands the buyer a re-trade.

**Bankers**: worth it above roughly 25-50M in enterprise value, for competitive processes, or when the CEO cannot afford the bandwidth. Fees are a percentage of deal value with a minimum, plus expenses; negotiate the tail period (their claim on a deal after termination) and exclude the buyers you already know. Below that size, experienced M&A counsel plus your board network is usually the better trade.

## Terms Beyond Price

- **Cash versus stock**: acquirer stock is a bet on their outcome with none of your control, and private-company stock may be unsellable for years. Ask about registration rights, lockups, and whether there is a liquidity path.
- **Escrow/holdback**: 10-15% for 12-18 months is convention; representation and warranty insurance can reduce or replace it, and is standard above certain deal sizes.
- **Earnouts**: assume they do not pay in full. Every earnout metric depends on decisions the buyer will control after close. If you accept one, tie it to a metric you can still influence and write in operating covenants — headcount, budget, and pricing authority.
- **Retention and employee outcomes**: what happens to unvested options, whether there is acceleration, and how the retention pool is allocated. Your team's outcome is a term you negotiate, not an afterthought — and single-trigger acceleration for everyone is a term the buyer will resist because it deletes their retention.
- **Founder commitments**: employment terms, vesting of the retention grant, non-compete scope and duration. Two years is a real cost; price it or narrow it.
- **Non-solicit and no-shop**: the no-shop period is your leverage handed over. Keep it as short as they will accept, with an outside date.

## The Human Part

- Your team learns at signing, not before, but you decide the message before you sign (→ SKILL.md Quick Reference: communication). Their first two questions are: do I have a job, and what happens to my equity. Have both answers ready.
- Key employees may be asked to sign new employment agreements as a closing condition. Find out who and tell them early enough that the deal does not hinge on a surprise conversation.
- Deals die. Roughly a third of signed LOIs do not close — run the company as if it will not close, because that is also your only leverage.
- The reference you leave with the acquirer's team and your own is what you take to the next company.
