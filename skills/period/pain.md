# Period Pain — Relief That Works and the Line to a Clinician

Advise-mode: evidence-ranked comfort measures she can act on today, plus the pattern that means the pain is not "just cramps."

## Primary vs Secondary — the Distinction That Matters

| Pattern | Reads as | Move |
|---------|----------|------|
| Cramping days 1-3, started within a few years of first periods, responds at least partly to OTC relief | Primary dysmenorrhea (prostaglandin-driven) | Relief playbook below |
| Pain that is new or worsening years after periods began; pain on days without bleeding; pain with sex or with bowel movements during the period | Secondary pattern — endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids | Track every episode with cycle day and severity; clinician with the log |
| Pain OTC does not touch, or that stops daily activity | Red Flags row (SKILL.md) | Flag; recommend evaluation |
| Sudden severe one-sided pain | Torsion/ectopic Red Flags row | Emergency, not tracking |

Endometriosis affects roughly 1 in 10 women of reproductive age and is commonly diagnosed years after symptoms start — a clean tracked pain log is the single best accelerator she can bring to an appointment.

## Relief Playbook (evidence-ranked)

1. **NSAIDs, started early.** First-line by Cochrane evidence. Start at the first sign of bleeding or cramps — before pain peaks — because NSAIDs block new prostaglandin production and do nothing about prostaglandins already released. Consistent label-dose through the heavy days beats chasing established pain. Ibuprofen, naproxen, and mefenamic acid all work; paracetamol/acetaminophen is measurably weaker for cramping (fallback when NSAIDs are contraindicated — reflux, ulcers, some asthma: that check is hers or her clinician's).
2. **Continuous low-level heat.** Comparable to ibuprofen in trials (Akin) and stacks with it. Patch or bottle on the lower abdomen through the worst hours.
3. **Regular exercise.** Moderate Cochrane-grade evidence for lower pain across cycles — a between-periods habit, not a mid-cramp intervention.
4. **TENS.** Evidence-supported option when NSAIDs are off the table.
5. **Hormonal contraception.** Effective for dysmenorrhea, prescription-level — a clinician conversation her pain log makes shorter.

Small-trial or harmless-at-best tier: ginger, magnesium, dietary tweaks. Fine to try, never presented as equivalent to the ranked list.

## Mid-Cycle Pain

One-sided twinge lasting hours to ~2 days near predicted ovulation = mittelschmerz, benign and actually useful as a soft ovulation signal (log it). Severe, sudden, or accompanied by fever or vomiting → emergency row, not mittelschmerz.

## Tracking Pain Well

- Log: cycle day, location (midline vs one-sided), severity (mild / moderate / severe as defined in `symptoms.md`), what she took and whether it worked.
- "Did the NSAID work?" is a data point: primary dysmenorrhea usually responds at least partly; repeated non-response across cycles strengthens the secondary pattern above.
- Severity drift across cycles (each period worse than the last) is a sustained shift — surface it (SKILL.md rule 6).
